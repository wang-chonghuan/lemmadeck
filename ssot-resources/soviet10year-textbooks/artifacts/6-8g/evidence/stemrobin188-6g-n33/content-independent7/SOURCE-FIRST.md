## Independent definitions and source findings

Let `N` denote the ordinary neutral plane background: incidence and order,
line/segment extension, half-plane separation and Pasch/crossbar, congruence
including SAS, angle copying and angle order, and the usual elementary
midpoint/perpendicular constructions. It excludes the parallel axiom and the
triangle angle-sum theorem. This is not spherical/elliptic global geometry.
Lines below are distinct coplanar full lines; triangles are nondegenerate.
Parallel means disjoint lines in that plane.

Equivalence of propositions `P` and `Q`, relative to the same background `N`,
means both `N + P => Q` and `N + Q => P`. Neither implication is obtained just
by renaming symbols. A stronger background must not be silently included in a
purported neutral proof.

### D2: actual negation/quantifier mismatch in T04

The uniqueness axiom has logical form:

`for every line a and every P outside a: at most one parallel through P`.

Its bare logical negation is:

`there exist a line a and a point P outside a with at least two distinct parallels through P`.

At `p0156#8`, the source instead calls the assertion "if P is not on a, at least
two parallels can be drawn through P" its negation. Read as the universal
hyperbolic replacement, this is stronger than the displayed existential
negation; read as one fixed counterexample, its missing existential scope needs
to be made explicit. A theorem transferring one counterexample to arbitrary
point-line pairs under suitable neutral assumptions is not a law of negation,
and is not proved in these 12 paragraphs. This is a logical exposition defect,
not a finding that hyperbolic geometry itself is contradictory.

The paragraph's `B` names the non-Euclidean replacement; in 441, `B` names the

## Independent solution 445: strict exterior-angle theorem

Source: `p0159#4`.
Required conclusion: each adjacent supplementary exterior angle at `A` is
strictly greater than **both** `angle ABC` and `angle ACB`, separately.
"任一个" is not an either/or choice of only one opposite angle.

Extend `BA` beyond `A` to `D`; the exterior angle considered is `angle CAD`.
Also extend `CA` beyond `A` to `G`. Then `angle BAG` and `angle CAD` are
vertical angles, so they are equal without a parallel assumption.

First prove `angle CAD > angle ACB`:

1. Take the midpoint `M` of `AC`. Extend `BM` beyond `M` to `N1`, choosing
   `MN1 = MB`. The orders are `A-M-C` and `B-M-N1`.
2. In triangles `CMB` and `AMN1`, `CM = AM`, `MB = MN1`, and
   `angle CMB = angle AMN1` are vertical angles. SAS gives congruence, hence
   `angle ACB = angle MCB = angle MAN1`.
3. The ray `AN1` is strictly inside exterior angle `CAD`, by neutral order:
   `B` and `N1` are on opposite sides of line `AC`, since their segment crosses
   it at `M`. Also `N1` and `C` are on the same side of line `AB`: `M` and `C`
   are on that side, and `M,N1` lie on the same ray from `B` of a line that
   meets `AB` only at `B`. Those two half-plane conditions describe the interior
   of angle `CAD`. Neither boundary contains `N1`.
4. Thus `angle MAN1` is a proper positive part of `angle CAD`. Angle order
   gives the strict inequality `angle CAD > angle ACB`.

Now prove `angle CAD > angle ABC` independently:

1. Take the midpoint `E` of `AB`. Extend `CE` beyond `E` to `F`, choosing
   `EF = EC`. The orders are `A-E-B` and `C-E-F`.
2. Triangles `BEC` and `AEF` have `BE = AE`, `EC = EF`, and equal vertical
   included angles `BEC` and `AEF`. By SAS,
   `angle ABC = angle EBC = angle EAF`.
3. `F` is opposite `C` across line `AB`. It is on the same side of line `AC`
   as `B`: `E` and `B` are on that side and `E,F` are on the same ray from `C`
   of a line meeting `AC` only at `C`. Hence `AF` is strictly inside
   exterior angle `BAG`, not on either boundary.
4. Therefore `angle BAG > angle EAF = angle ABC`. Vertical-angle equality
   yields `angle CAD > angle ABC` as well.

Both constructions use midpoint existence, segment extension, SAS, vertical
angles, half-plane/order facts, and strict angle order only. No parallel is
drawn, no parallel-angle converse is assumed, and no triangle angle sum or
exterior-angle equality with the sum of opposite angles is used.

## Neutral lemma: two perpendiculars to the same line

Let distinct lines `u` and `v` be perpendicular to `c` at distinct points
`H` and `Q`. If `u,v` met at `R`, triangle `HQR` would have right angles at
`H` and `Q`. At `H`, an adjacent supplementary exterior angle is also a right
angle. By 445 it must be strictly larger than the right interior angle at `Q`,
which is impossible. Thus `u || v`. This is neutral; there is no angle-sum
argument and no parallel axiom in this lemma.

The ordinary neutral construction of the perpendicular from an outside point
to a line, followed by the unique perpendicular at that point, therefore
constructs at least one parallel through the outside point. This existence
must not be silently conflated with axiom `A`, which asserts at most one.

## Independent solution 441: both directions of equivalence

Source: `p0158#6`. In this exercise only:

- `A`: through any point there is at most one line parallel to a given line.
- `B`: if `a` is perpendicular to `c` and `b` intersects `c` without being
  perpendicular to it, then `a` intersects `b`.

Use the same background `N` in both directions.

### `N + A => B`

Let `H = a intersection c` and `Q = b intersection c`. If `H = Q`,
`a,b` already intersect, so suppose `H != Q`. Construct `v` perpendicular
to `c` at `Q`. The neutral double-perpendicular lemma gives `v || a`.

Assume for contradiction `b` does not meet `a`. Then `b || a` and `v || a`
are both through the outside point `Q`. **Here and only here the parallel
uniqueness axiom `A` is used**: it forces `b = v`, contrary to the premise
that `b` is not perpendicular to `c`. Consequently `a` and `b` intersect.

### `N + B => A`

Take any `P` outside a given line `a`. Construct the perpendicular `c` from
`P` to `a`; at `P` construct the unique perpendicular `v` to `c`. The neutral
lemma gives `v || a`, providing a parallel without assuming `A`.

Let `b` be any parallel to `a` through `P`. It intersects `c` at `P`. If
`b` were not perpendicular to `c`, **the hypothesized proposition `B`**
would force it to intersect `a`, a contradiction. Every such `b` must
therefore be perpendicular to `c` at `P`, and neutral perpendicular uniqueness
forces `b = v`. At most one parallel passes through `P`, as `A` requires.
For a point on `a`, no distinct disjoint line through it exists, so the
"arbitrary point" formulation adds no exceptional case.

The two implications prove the asserted equivalence, not a neutral proof of
either Euclidean assertion. This proof does not confuse the exercise's `B`
with T04's hyperbolic `B`.

## Independent solution 442: genuinely dependent examples

Source: `p0159#1`. Three actual theorem examples and their dependencies:

1. **Distinct parallel lines cut by a transversal have equal corresponding
   angles.** At the second intersection, copy the first angle to form a line.
   The neutral sufficient criterion 444 makes that new line parallel to the
   first. Axiom `A` forces it to be the already given parallel, so its angle
   equals the copied angle. The uniqueness step is essential.
2. **Every Euclidean triangle has interior-angle sum two right angles.**
   Construct a parallel through one vertex to the opposite side. The
   equal-corresponding/alternate-angle theorem just described moves the other
   two interior angles to that vertex; together they partition a straight
   angle. The angle-transfer theorem depends on `A`. This equality is not a
   premise of 445.
3. **Two distinct lines parallel to one common third line are parallel to
   each other.** If the two met at `P`, they would be two distinct parallels
   to the third through the same outside point. Axiom `A` forbids that.
   Therefore they do not meet.

These are not merely familiar Euclidean-looking statements without a dependency
explanation. In particular, the sufficient direction of 444 and the strict
inequality in 445 are not valid examples for 442.

## Independent solution 443: genuinely neutral examples

Source: `p0159#2`. Examples with proofs using no parallel axiom:

1. **Vertical angles are equal.** Each is supplementary to the same adjacent
   angle; subtracting equal angles from one straight angle gives equality.
2. **An isosceles triangle has equal base angles.** Bisect its vertex angle;
   the interior bisector meets the base by the neutral crossbar/order property.
   The two resulting triangles have equal legs, the common bisector segment,
   and equal included angles. SAS gives equal base angles.
3. **A triangle's exterior angle is strictly greater than each remote interior
   angle.** The two complete constructions in 445 prove this using only SAS
   and order.
4. **Distinct perpendiculars to one line are parallel.** The neutral lemma
   above proves this by 445, not by a two-right-angle/angle-sum calculation.

The corresponding-angle sufficient criterion in 444 is also neutral. The
triangle angle-sum equality, the necessary corresponding-angle condition for
already parallel lines, and transitivity of arbitrary parallel lines are not
being presented as neutral examples.

## Independent solution 444: sufficient, not silently necessary

Source: `p0159#3`.
Conclusion required: equal corresponding angles **imply** parallel lines.

Let the transversal `t` meet distinct lines `l,m` at distinct points `U,V`,
with the forward direction of `t` from `U` toward `V`. If the given
corresponding angles are equal, their corresponding supplements are also equal.
We may therefore select either of the two matching rays on `l,m` without
losing equality.

Suppose `l,m` intersect at `O`. Then `O` is off `t`. Choose the matching
line rays on the side of `t` containing `O`; they are `UO` and `VO`.
The angle between forward `t` and `UO` at `U` is the interior angle
`angle OUV` of triangle `UVO`. The corresponding angle between forward `t`
beyond `V` and `VO` at `V` is an exterior angle of that triangle. The
hypothesis says these two angles are equal, whereas 445 says that exterior
angle is **strictly greater** than the remote interior angle at `U`.
This contradiction shows `l,m` cannot meet: they are parallel.

If the chosen orientation of the source pair is opposite, take the
corresponding supplements first; no assumption about a drawn intersection
side is needed. Incidence excludes `O` on `t`, since `U,V` are distinct.

This proof uses only `N + 445`. It never invokes the statement "parallel
lines have equal corresponding angles" to prove its converse. That latter
statement is the **necessary** direction, proved in 442 using `A`. Thus:

- Equal corresponding angles are a sufficient parallel criterion in neutral
  geometry.
- In Euclidean geometry they are also necessary, using the parallel axiom.
- Calling the two directions interchangeable without recording that dependency
  would be circular in this exercise.
