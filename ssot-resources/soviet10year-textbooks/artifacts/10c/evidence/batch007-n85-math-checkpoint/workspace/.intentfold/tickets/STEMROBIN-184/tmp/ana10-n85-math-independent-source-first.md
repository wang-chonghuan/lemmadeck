## Source-definition-based mathematical derivations

### Sine, variation, and minimal period

The unit-circle point convention used for g3 is

```text
P_t=(cos t,sin t), t real; C={(u,v):u^2+v^2=1}.
```

Consequently its ordinate is the sine value, its abscissa is not `t`, and `t` and `t+2\pi m` name the same circle point. This is a mathematical interpretation of the original unit-circle task context, not a new provided diagram.

The ordinate is between `-1` and `1`, both attained. Reflection sends `P_t` to `P_{-t}=(\cos t,-\sin t)`, so sine is odd. One full turn produces period `2\pi`. Zero ordinate gives precisely the two horizontal-axis circle points, hence all real zeros are `t=\pi m`.

For completeness, if `T>0` is any period, setting `x=0` gives `\sin T=0`, so `T=\pi m`. Setting `x=\pi/2` gives `\sin(\pi/2+T)=1`, so `\cos T=1`; thus `m` is even. The least positive possibility is `2\pi`.

Using P002, `(\sin x)'=\cos x`. In `[-\pi,\pi]`, cosine vanishes only at `-\pi/2` and `\pi/2`, is negative on the two outside open pieces, and positive on the central open piece. This proves the table's monotonicity with its endpoint values. Translating these intervals by `2\pi m`, with integer `m`, proves all the increase/decrease and extremum families in P062-P065.

On `(0,\pi/2]`, sine increases from `0` to `1`; on `[\pi/2,\pi)`, it decreases from `1` to `0`. Therefore it is positive throughout `(0,\pi)`. Oddness, or the lower circle semicircle, gives negativity throughout `(\pi,2\pi)`. Integer-period translation gives all positive and negative lobes. This derivation is independent of the defective literal qualifier in P059.

### Principal inverse and its values

The restricted function

```text
f:[-pi/2,pi/2] -> [-1,1], f(t)=sin t
```

is continuous, strictly increasing, and maps its endpoints to `-1` and `1`. Thus it is one-to-one and onto that closed range and has the unique inverse `\arcsin`.

For every real `a` in `[-1,1]`, `\arcsin a` is the unique angle `\alpha\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]` with `\sin\alpha=a`. The closed endpoints are essential: `\arcsin(\pm1)=\pm\pi/2`. There is no real inverse-sine value outside `[-1,1]`.

If `a<b`, the corresponding inverse angles are ordered because f is strictly increasing, proving increase of the inverse. Oddness follows from uniqueness: if `\sin\alpha=a`, then `\sin(-\alpha)=-a` and `-\alpha` is still in the principal range.

The identity `\sin(\arcsin a)=a` holds throughout `[-1,1]`. The reverse identity `\arcsin(\sin t)=t` holds only when `t\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]`; global inversion of sine would be false.

For degree conversion, choose `\beta` in the closed degree range `[-90,90]` and set `y=\beta\pi/180`. Then the same angle has measure `\beta` degrees or `y` radians. Since `y` belongs to the principal interval, `\sin y=a` implies `y=\arcsin a`. The units cannot be dropped before conversion.

### Complete real equation families and endpoint cases

Let `a` be real.

1. `|a|>1`: no real solution.
2. `a=1`: `x=\pi/2+2\pi m`, integer `m`.
3. `a=-1`: `x=-\pi/2+2\pi m`, integer `m`.
4. `|a|<1`: put `\alpha=\arcsin a`, so `-\pi/2<\alpha<\pi/2`.

For the fourth case, on `[-\pi/2,\pi/2]` strict increase gives exactly one root, `\alpha`. On `[\pi/2,3\pi/2]` strict decrease gives exactly one root. If `x` is in the second interval, then

```text
pi/2 <= x <= 3pi/2
-pi/2 <= pi-x <= pi/2.
```

The order reverses under subtraction, and the two interval endpoints exchange their roles. Since `\sin x=\sin(\pi-x)`, uniqueness in the principal interval gives `\pi-x=\alpha`, hence the second root `\pi-\alpha`. Translating by the period gives

```text
x=alpha+2pi*m OR x=pi-alpha+2pi*m, m integer.
```

Each representative lies in the chosen closed period window and is unique on its half; every real root can be translated into that window. Thus there are no missing branches. For `|a|<1`, the two sets are distinct modulo `2\pi`: equality would require `2\alpha=\pi+2\pi j`, which is impossible in `(-\pi,\pi)`.

Writing `k=2m` or `k=2m+1` gives the unified form

```text
x=(-1)^k*arcsin(a)+pi*k, k integer, for -1<=a<=1.
```

At `a=1`, the branches both give `\pi/2+2\pi m`. At `a=-1`, the second branch `3\pi/2+2\pi m` is the first branch `-\pi/2+2\pi(m+1)`, so they again coincide as sets. At `a=0`, the two branch sets are distinct even and odd multiples of `\pi`; their union simplifies to all multiples.

The same derivation supplies the affine-argument rule needed for exercises 143, 144, and 146. For fixed nonzero real `b` and real `c`,

```text
sin(b*x+c)=a, |a|<=1:
x = ((-1)^k*arcsin(a)+pi*k-c)/b, k integer.
```

Equivalently, the two branches are `(alpha-c+2\pi m)/b` and `(\pi-alpha-c+2\pi m)/b`. If `|a|>1`, there are no real solutions. A zero coefficient would be a different case: every real `x` if `\sin c=a`, otherwise none. No n85 task has that zero coefficient.
