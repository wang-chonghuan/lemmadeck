## Source 1: Instrument And Principle

To measure force, use a dynamometer. In the lesson's proportional working regime,
increasing a spring's deformation by a factor increases its elastic force by the
same factor. With deformation x measured from the unloaded position, the relation
can be written F = kx for the same spring, with k constant in that regime.
Therefore deformation ratios equal force ratios. It is the deformation, not the
spring's total length or the pointer's distance from the top of the backing,
that carries the force information. The pointer indicates this deformation
against a force-calibrated scale. A constant position or force without a
deformation response would not provide this measurement.

The instrument is not restricted to gravity: the selected prose also names
friction and elastic force. Use and range must be appropriate to the actual force.
The written response should identify the instrument and explain the causal
measurement principle, rather than merely name a spring.

## Source 2: Make And Calibrate The Simple Instrument

Attach the upper end of a spring to the paper-covered backing. Attach the thin
rod and lower load hook to its lower end, and attach the pointer to the upper
part of that moving rod. The approved figure shows a lower guide fixed near the
backing bottom; the rod travels through it. Do not fix the pointer to the backing
or move the guide with the load.

With no load, mark the pointer's position as zero. Apply a known reference force;
after the apparatus settles, mark the new pointer position with that force's
value. The lesson uses a hanging reference mass 1/9.8 kg to establish 1 N, then
approximately twice and three times this mass for 2 N and 3 N. The deformation
and the force rise in the same proportion, so these whole-newton marks establish
the scale. Known nonzero loads cannot replace the unloaded zero reference;
arbitrary uniformly spaced marks without a force reference do not establish N.

Numerical precision matters. The seed's 1/9.8 kg corresponding to 1 N implies
its working conversion factor 9.8 N/kg; this factor is inferred from that given
relation, not imported from another lesson. The exact reference mass under that
model is 5/49 kg, approximately 102.040816 g. The printed integer masses are
rounded classroom values:

- 102 g gives 0.102 x 9.8 = 0.9996 N, approximately 1 N.
- 204 g gives 0.204 x 9.8 = 1.9992 N, approximately 2 N.
- 306 g gives 0.306 x 9.8 = 2.9988 N, approximately 3 N.

Thus neither the visible 102 g label nor the source's "即" warrants claiming
that an integer-gram mass produces an exact integer-newton force. A choice can
use explicitly given ideal 1 N/2 N/3 N force references, or the rounded mass
procedure with its approximation stated, but must not equate the two exactly.
The written task includes producing an assembly and describing a coherent
calibration procedure; selecting a proposed assembly cannot demonstrate doing it.

## Source 3: Establish 0.1 N Graduations

Take the already calibrated positions for two adjacent whole-newton values,
such as 0 and 1 N. Divide that spatial interval into ten equal intervals because
the spring deformation and force are proportional in the stated regime:
1 N / 10 = 0.1 N per interval. Place nine new interior marks; the two original
endpoints plus those nine marks give eleven positions and ten gaps.
Repeat within 1 to 2 N, 2 to 3 N, and further whole-newton intervals as needed.

Dividing the entire 0-to-3 N span into only ten equal intervals instead gives
3 N / 10 = 0.3 N per interval. To obtain 0.1 N over that full span requires
thirty equal intervals and thirty-one total scale lines, with twenty-nine interior positions (including the existing 1 N and 2 N lines); with 0, 1, 2 and 3 N already marked, only twenty-seven new lines are needed. Choosing ten marks
including both endpoints would instead create nine gaps, not ten. A physical
graduation task requires accurate placement; knowing the interval count does
not establish manual spacing skill.

## Source 4: Grip And Traction Uses

A grip dynamometer measures hand grip force. In the approved figure the hand
compresses the oval spring and the indication mechanism turns a pointer.
A traction dynamometer measures large pulling forces, such as a tractor pulling
a trailer; the approved figure places it in that load path. The lesson says
this instrument class can measure forces of tens of thousands of newtons.
That capacity statement does not specify this illustrated dial's unit or scale
multiplier, and neither image supplies an exact force reading.

Instrument selection follows the measured force and suitable range, not merely
whether a pointer is straight or rotating. A force instrument does not directly
give a tractor's speed or a hand's mass. Explaining the two uses and their
different load application remains substantive; picking an existing explanation
does not demonstrate generating one independently.
