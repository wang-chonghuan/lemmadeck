## Source 1

Written task: independently supply observations and experiments that indicate
friction exists, not merely name a term.

A block sliding on a horizontal table slows after the push ends. If the
continuing horizontal resistance is at the contact surface (air resistance and
other horizontal resistances explicitly negligible), the slowing is evidence
of a contact force opposing the slide. Sliding one's palm across a surface
also gives a directly felt opposing contact resistance; this is qualitative
observation rather than a measurement of a friction coefficient.

A controlled experiment can use the same block and load, the same initial
sliding speed, horizontal surfaces, and unchanged other conditions. Repeat
slides on different surface finishes and compare the slowing or stopping
distances. A repeatable change tied to the contact surface supports the
surface-friction explanation. A rougher surface giving a shorter stopping
distance can be an explicitly observed result, not an asserted universal law
about every possible material. Different unrecorded initial speeds invalidate
that comparison. Slowdown by itself, without isolating competing resistance,
does not prove that all resistance is surface friction.

This solution supplies an observation and a practicable comparison, while
distinguishing evidence, control of variables, and quantitative measurement.
It does not invoke the following lesson's dynamometer measurement method.

## Source 2

Written task: explain what kind of force is called friction in the supplied
lesson.

Use this lesson's moving-contact definition: when a body moves on another
body's surface, the contact produces a force opposed to that motion. The
motion for a sliding contact is the body's motion relative to the contacted
surface, not necessarily its motion relative to the ground. If a board moves
right and a contacting block also moves right but more slowly, the block
slides left relative to the board; the board's sliding friction on it points
right. Moving in the same ground direction does not imply zero relative
slide. Being pressed onto a horizontal board does not make the sliding
friction a downward force.

This is the book's restricted moving-contact definition, not a claim that all
static friction or every rolling-resistance mechanism in modern physics must
be opposite the body's ground velocity.

## Source 3

Written task: give both causes discussed in the lesson and explain their roles.

One cause is irregularity of the contacting surfaces. Protrusions and grooves
can interlock and oppose relative movement. A surface that looks smooth may
still have small irregularities. For rough finished surfaces, the lesson
identifies this geometric mechanism as the main cause.

The other cause is attraction between molecules at contacting surfaces.
Very well polished contacting surfaces can bring some molecules very close,
making their attraction appreciable. Suppressing visible roughness therefore
does not imply zero friction. Conversely, the molecular cause does not erase
the interlocking mechanism in a rough contact. These are two explanations in
the supplied model, not a demand to assign a numerical fraction to each.

## Source 4

Written task: state the effect of lubrication and give its explanation.

In the lesson's lubrication situation, a film separates the solid rubbing
surfaces and prevents their direct contact. The layers sliding past one
another are then lubricant layers. The liquid-layer friction described in
the lesson is smaller than the solid-surface friction, so lubrication reduces
the opposing force; it does not make all friction vanish.

The film is not permanent sanding or removal of the solids' protrusions.
It changes the contact between surfaces, not a stated mass, load, speed,
numerical coefficient or force ratio. The book's ice-skate water-film example
and oil applications illustrate this mechanism; no temperature or numerical
reduction is supplied, so none can be calculated or inferred from the drawing.

The prepared concept figure is fig-75, with two magnified schematic panels.
Its instructional use is to distinguish touching irregular solids from
solids separated by a film, not to measure roughness or film thickness.

## Source 5

Written task: name the kinds of friction taught here.

The lesson introduces sliding friction and rolling friction. A useful answer
also distinguishes the underlying motions: sliding on the other surface
versus rolling on it. This is an account of this lesson's two categories,
not an exhaustive modern taxonomy excluding static friction.

## Source 6

Written task: say under what conditions sliding friction is discussed.

A body is contacting and sliding along another body's surface. Mere contact
is insufficient; relative sliding is the distinction. A sled moving over
snow is a book example. A round wheel with its rotation locked and its tire
skidding along the ground has a sliding contact; circular shape alone does
not turn the skid into rolling.

No independent measurement, coefficient or numerical magnitude is requested.
The definition does not establish the forces on a stationary contact.

## Source 7

Written task: explain what friction is called rolling friction.

When a body rolls on another body's surface rather than sliding on it, the
friction discussed in this lesson is called rolling friction. A log or
barrel rolling on the ground is an example. Rotation alone is insufficient:
a barrel spinning while suspended clear of the ground has no barrel-ground
contact and so cannot have the ground rolling friction described here.

Use the supplied elementary category for rolling bodies; do not replace it
with an unprojected tire-contact/static-friction model. No claim about the
relative magnitudes of rolling and sliding friction is available in this
seed, and the next lesson's same-load comparison is not imported.
