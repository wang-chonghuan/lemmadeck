## Source 1: How to Measure Friction

Attach a suitable spring dynamometer to the wooden block on a level board or table. Before measuring, set its unloaded zero and read its scale in force units. Pull the block horizontally so that it slides in a straight line at a steady speed, rather than reading the starting peak or a period of acceleration. Take readings during this steady sliding stage; repeat comparable trials if needed.

The dynamometer directly measures its spring's elastic pull on the block, not the friction force acting through the contact. For this horizontal measurement, the relevant horizontal forces are the pull T in the direction of travel and sliding friction f in the opposite direction. Under the stated steady straight-line condition and with no other horizontal force, their resultant is zero, so T - f = 0 and their magnitudes satisfy f = T. Thus the steady dynamometer reading gives the magnitude of friction indirectly.

The two forces have opposite directions; equality of their magnitudes does not identify them as the same force. The statement about two forces concerns the horizontal force balance, not the literal total number of forces on the block: gravity and the contact support still act vertically.

If the block accelerates, T - f is not zero and the pull cannot simply be equated to friction. If it has not yet started moving, the reading concerns static contact resistance rather than the intended steady sliding measurement. A tilted pull can also change how strongly the block presses on the surface; keep the pull horizontal for the intended comparison. No coefficient of friction, block mass, dynamometer graduation value or numerical result can be inferred from the supplied text.

## Source 2: Demonstrate Dependence on the Force Pressing the Surfaces Together

First measure the steady sliding friction of the block alone using the preceding horizontal, uniform-motion method. Then add a securely placed weight to the block, without changing the board, the block's contacting face, the surface condition or the direction of the horizontal pull. Repeat the measurement at a comparable steady speed with the same instrument.

The added load increases the force pressing the block toward the contacting surface. In the lesson's experiment, the steady reading is larger after adding the load; because the steady horizontal reading equals friction in each trial, this supports the conclusion that increasing the pressing force increases the measured friction when the other relevant conditions are held fixed.

The lesson also permits pressing down with a hand. That alternative requires a controlled downward press without an extra horizontal force and without changing the contact surface; a load is a clearer reproducible way to vary the pressing condition. A vigorous forward push with the hand would introduce another horizontal force and invalidate the simple reading interpretation.

Do not change surface material/roughness, contacting face or motion type at the same time as the load and then attribute the entire reading difference to pressing force. Do not use an accelerating trial in one condition and steady sliding in the other. The original task asks for an experimental demonstration of a dependence, not proof of exact proportionality. The supplied observations do not justify a numerical coefficient, an exact doubling law or a universal formula for every material.

## Source 3: Compare Sliding and Rolling at the Same Load

Use the same block and supported load on the same level board for both measurements. First pull it horizontally in steady straight-line sliding and record the steady dynamometer reading. Then place two suitable round rods beneath the block as described by the lesson, keep the supported load unchanged and pull horizontally so that the rods roll rather than remaining locked and skidding. Record the steady reading during the rolling arrangement.

Keep the board condition, chosen loading, instrument, pull direction and comparable steady-motion procedure fixed. Changing from direct sliding contact to rolling on rods is the intended treatment, not an accidental additional change to be ignored. The apparatus must allow rolling; merely putting immobile rods under the block does not establish a rolling-friction trial.

Under these classroom conditions, the lesson reports that the steady reading for rolling is smaller than for sliding. Since each reading measures the horizontal pull required to maintain the corresponding steady motion, the experiment demonstrates the intended same-load comparison: rolling resistance in this arrangement is less than sliding friction in the direct-sliding arrangement.

It does not demonstrate that every arbitrarily chosen rolling system has less resistance than every unrelated sliding system. Using a lighter loaded block in the rolling trial, a different board, or a changing-speed trial would not isolate the intended same-load motion-type comparison. The text provides no actual readings or their ratio; they must be measured, not invented.
