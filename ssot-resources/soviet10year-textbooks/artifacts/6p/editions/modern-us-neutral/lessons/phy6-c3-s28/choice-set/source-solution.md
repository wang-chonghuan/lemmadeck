## Source1: Construct An Example

An example is tearing a sheet of paper into two pieces. Molecules on opposite sides of the tear were held together by attraction; applying a separating force pulls those neighboring parts apart and overcomes their attraction at the tear.

This differs from merely translating the intact sheet without changing its internal separations, and from compressing material so that short-range repulsion resists closer approach. The required source ability is independently producing an example and connecting it to internal molecular separation, not selecting a term.

## Source2: Why Molecules Cannot Be Packed Arbitrarily Close

At sufficiently small molecular distances, repulsion increases rapidly and becomes greater than attraction. Its net action opposes further approach. Therefore attraction does not make the molecules collapse together without resistance.

Attraction and repulsion coexist: dominant repulsion does not imply absent attraction, and balanced forces do not imply zero individual forces. The seed uses a qualitative molecular model; it supplies no hard radius, calibrated force or exact shortest distance.

## Source3: Complete Distance-Dependence

As the distance decreases, both attraction and repulsion increase, but repulsion increases faster.

| Stage | Distance and relative strengths | Net tendency |
|---|---|---|
| a / upper row | Relatively farther apart; attraction is small, repulsion still smaller | Attraction dominates |
| b / middle row | Closer, with equal attraction and repulsion | The two forces balance |
| c / lower row | Closer still; both larger, repulsion greater | Repulsion opposes closer approach |

Equality at one distance is not equality at every distance. One cannot infer that attraction stays constant, that it decreases during compression, or that it grows faster than repulsion.

This is a qualitative strength comparison, not a request for numerical force values or a proportional arrow-length measurement.

## Source4: Steel Cable Contraction

In the recoverable deformation situation described by the selected definition, stretching increases molecular spacing. Attraction then exceeds repulsion. Removing the external stretching force does not remove those internal interactions; their imbalance draws the molecules closer and the cable contracts.

At restored equilibrium, attraction and repulsion can both remain present and balance. The source explanation presupposes recoverable stretching; it does not warrant a claim that a broken or permanently deformed cable must return to its original length. A choice stem may explicitly retain this recoverable-deformation condition rather than silently generalizing.

The corresponding compression example in block7 is consistent: reduced spacing makes repulsion dominate, and releasing the compression lets the cable expand. It supplies the contrast but is not a fifth original source question.
