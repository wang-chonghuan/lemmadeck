## Source 1: Why Gravity Does Not Make All Molecules Settle

Task: explain why attraction to Earth does not make all atmospheric gas
molecules collect on the ground.

Gas molecules undergo continual irregular thermal motion, rather than
remaining stationary under gravity. In the lesson's simplified account,
motion and gravity jointly maintain a gas distribution around Earth.
Gravity acts; it is not absent or canceled by a separate permanent upward
force on each molecule. Molecules are not all motionless at a fixed height,
nor do they all cease random motion when moving downward.

The relevant distinction is dynamic molecular motion versus a population
of stationary particles falling to one surface. A choice can test this
explanation, but cannot prove that a learner can independently express it.
No numerical calculation or individual trajectory is requested or supplied.

## Source 2: Why Most Molecules Do Not Escape

Task: explain the other boundary of atmospheric retention while preserving
the source's qualified simplified model.

The lesson compares molecular speeds with the idealized escape speed near
Earth's surface, considering Earth's gravity only:
11.2 km/s = 11200 m/s. It says the average molecular speed is far smaller,
so the overwhelming majority remain gravitationally bound near Earth.
Random motion does not mean unlimited speed or elimination of gravity.

The average comparison is the lesson's heuristic population explanation;
it is not a proof that every molecule's speed is below the threshold or
that no molecule can escape under any height/condition. The escape speed
is not a universal molecular speed ceiling at every location. There is
no supplied distribution from which to calculate a fraction escaping.
Do not turn this into a numerical probability or exact retention theorem.

Sources 1 and 2 form one coherent two-sided atmospheric-retention task:
why the population neither all settles nor all disperses. A single complete
paired explanation can preserve both distinctions without two nearly
duplicate isolated term questions.

## Source 3: Density Versus Height

Task: describe how atmospheric density changes with increasing height.

The lesson describes a rapid overall decrease: higher air is more rarefied.
Its approximate examples give rho(5.4 km) approximately rho(surface)/2
and rho(11 km) approximately rho(surface)/4. They are illustrative
approximate contexts, not exact universal ratios for every site/condition.
They do not define a linear law or give enough data for arbitrary-height
interpolation. Figure142 has no numerical height/density scale and cannot
independently yield those ratios by counting dots.

The outer atmosphere gradually becomes extremely tenuous, without a
sharp boundary at one assigned height. Do not infer a sudden zero-density
edge from the frame of the illustration.

Definition4's static gas/container example is consistent context:
under gravity density and pressure are slightly higher below, often
negligibly different in ordinary containers, but a large atmospheric
height range is different. This contextual paragraph is not an extra
selected exercise or a new quantitative question.

## Source g2-1: Pipette Experiment And Explanation

Task: perform insertion, closure, lifting and reopening, and explain the
retention/release using the actual source instrument.

For the ordinary water-like-liquid demonstration, start with the upper
mouth open and insert the lower part into liquid. Air can leave the upper
mouth as liquid enters. Close the upper mouth while the lower part is
still in the liquid, then lift the pipette. The source depicts the lifted
states, not the insertion step.

With the upper mouth sealed, free atmospheric air cannot replenish the
space above the retained liquid. A slight downward adjustment/expansion
reduces that trapped-gas pressure below outside atmospheric pressure;
the outside pressure at the open lower mouth can balance the internal
gas pressure plus the liquid column's hydrostatic contribution.
In a simplified constant-section treatment, ignoring surface-tension
corrections, p_out = p_top + rho*g*h. Thus p_top is below p_out for h>0.
The finger supplies the seal, not a direct upward mechanical hold on the
entire water column, and the trapped region need not be perfect vacuum.

Opening the upper mouth lets air enter. The upper gas pressure approaches
outside pressure, removing the pressure difference that retained the
column; gravity then drives outflow from the open lower tip.
This explains the source's right-hand lower liquid surface and drops.
No flow rate, retained volume or drop timing is supplied by the drawing.

Meaningful choice cognition is a complete procedure/mechanism judgment,
with both lifted tips explicitly not immersed. Recognizing such judgments
does not demonstrate handling the instrument, observing a real experiment
or independently writing its explanation.

## Source g2-2: Rubber-Bulb Dropper

Task: do a dripping experiment and explain the rubber-bulb apparatus.

One workable sequence is to squeeze the bulb to expel air, keep it
squeezed while placing the tip below the liquid surface, then release
it with the tip still immersed. Elastic recovery increases the enclosed
volume and temporarily lowers internal pressure. Atmospheric pressure on
the liquid outside pushes liquid through the tip into the tube.
It is not gravity lifting the liquid or an unexplained suction force
exerted directly on the liquid by the fingers.

After withdrawing a loaded dropper, gently squeeze the bulb. Its volume
decreases, increasing internal pressure and pushing liquid out of the
open tip; gentle control can produce drops. The physical drop shape also
involves surface tension, but this lesson supplies no size/rate data and
does not require a calibrated droplet model.

Figure144 gives one apparatus and one liquid state, not a procedure
sequence, detached-drop count or measured capacity. A method/mechanism
question must state its immersed/released/withdrawn conditions visibly.
Choice recognition loses actual manipulation, drop control, measurement,
observation and self-produced explanation.

## Source g2-3: Filling A Fountain Pen

Task: identify the physical phenomenon used when filling the pen.

In the customary atmospheric-pressure filling account, expelling air
and then enlarging a closed reservoir reduces pressure inside it while
the intake remains in ink. Outside atmospheric pressure on the ink
surface drives ink into the lower-pressure reservoir. This is the same
pressure-difference principle in a different instrument.

The written source does not specify a single pen mechanism. A compliant
ink sac or a piston can establish the pressure reduction; not every
modern cartridge/capillary filling design is identical. To make options
definite, a candidate can visibly specify the squeeze-sac example and
record the narrower apparatus scope. It must not assert that every pen
has that sac or that all filling phenomena exclude capillary effects.

Choosing a complete mechanism is more substantive than recognizing an
isolated term. The source asks for a phenomenon rather than a numerical
answer or an actual-operation certificate. Independent explanation and
application to unspecified pen mechanisms remain lost/narrowed capacity.

## Source 4: Automatic Poultry Waterer

Task: explain initial stable cessation, renewed flow when the trough level
falls enough to expose the bottle mouth, and the next cessation.

The bottle is inverted, closed except at its lower mouth, and contains
water plus an air space. Its mouth is initially slightly below the open
trough's water surface. External air cannot freely enter through that
submerged mouth. During adjustment some water can leave and the trapped
air expands, lowering its pressure.

Let H_in and H_out be the liquid heights above the same bottle-mouth
level inside the bottle and in the trough. At stable cessation:
p_air + rho*g*H_in = p_atm + rho*g*H_out.
The source geometry has H_in > H_out, hence p_air < p_atm.
The submerged opening is not a solid stopper, and no top vent is shown.
Equal mouth-level pressures, rather than equal liquid-surface heights
or zero bottle-air pressure, explain cessation.

When drinking lowers the trough water surface enough that the mouth
is exposed, air can enter through the mouth; the trapped-air pressure
can rise and bottle water flows out to replenish the trough.
After replenishment covers the mouth again, free air entry is cut off.
Further adjustment of trapped-air pressure can reestablish mouth-level
pressure balance and flow stops again. Do not claim that mere contact
with water immediately guarantees cessation before pressure balance.

The task has three parts of one feedback cycle, not three unrelated
answer slots. Figure145 depicts the stable submerged-mouth geometry,
so exposed-mouth behavior must be reasoned from the stated changed
condition, not claimed observed in a second image. No quantitative
flow/time/dimension, perfect-vacuum assumption, added valve or new
experiment is needed.
