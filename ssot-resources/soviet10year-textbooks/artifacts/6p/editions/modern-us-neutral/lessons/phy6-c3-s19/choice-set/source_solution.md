## Source Conventions

Force measurement means comparison with a force chosen as the unit.
The lesson uses newtons and kilonewtons, with 1 kilonewton = 1000 newtons.
The newton definition concerns a change of velocity, not an existing
velocity: a force causing a 1 kilogram body to change velocity by
1 metre/second in 1 second is 1 newton.

The lesson writes gravity as $F=gm$, with force in newtons, mass in
kilograms and $g=9.8$ newtons/kilogram. Weight is the force the body exerts
on a support or suspension because of Earth's attraction, not the
gravitational force acting on the body itself. In the textbook's weight
calculation context the support is horizontal and stationary relative
to Earth, and $P=F=gm$. Both conditions must be retained when using that
equality. No accelerating-support or unrestricted inclined-support
assertion is inferred from the supplied definition.

The complete allowed lesson also explicitly permits the integer
approximation $g\approx10$ newtons/kilogram in many calculations.
Consequently, absent a stated precision convention, a result computed
with 10 is not intrinsically a misconception or an unsupported answer.
Any choice task requiring one precise numeric result must visibly state
its selected $g$ and any rounding rule. The source values themselves
are not changed.

## 1: Measuring a Force

Task: explain what it means to measure a force.
Solution: choose a unit force and determine how many such units correspond
to the measured force; the resulting magnitude is expressed by a number
and a force unit. Comparing against a kilogram mass without specifying
its force does not by itself define a force measurement. Measuring only
a displacement or reading a mass number is not the complete comparison.
The conceptual distinction is the measured physical quantity and its
reference unit, rather than an independently named measuring instrument.

## 2: Force Unit

Task: name the force unit.
Solution: the lesson's force unit is the newton; the larger unit
kilonewton is 1000 newtons. Newtons apply to gravity and other forces,
including elastic force. Kilograms are units of mass, not force.
The answer need not exclude the correctly related kilonewton.

## 3: One Newton

Task: state what force counts as 1 newton.
Solution: a force which makes a body of mass 1 kilogram undergo a
velocity change of 1 metre/second during 1 second. All three quantities
and their units belong to the definition. A body merely moving steadily
at 1 metre/second has no such velocity change. The definition does not
mean moving a distance of 1 metre, holding a velocity, or giving every
mass the same velocity change.

## 4: Gravity of One Kilogram

Task: determine the gravitational force on a body of mass 1 kilogram.
Using the stated lesson coefficient:

$$F=9.8\ \text{牛顿/千克}\times1\ \text{千克}=9.8\ \text{牛顿}.$$

With the textbook's allowed integer approximation the result is
approximately 10 newtons. Neither answer justifies calling the mass
itself 9.8 kilograms or the force 1 kilogram. A unique choice calculation
can select 9.8 by explicitly specifying that coefficient.

## 5: Gravity of Arbitrary Mass

Task: explain the general calculation, not just return one value.
Convert a mass given in grams or tonnes to kilograms, then use $F=gm$.
The unit product is (newtons/kilogram) times kilograms = newtons.
With $g=9.8$ newtons/kilogram, a mass of $m$ kilograms has
$F=9.8m$ newtons. The source also permits the approximation
$F\approx10m$ newtons where an approximate result is appropriate.
Mass and gravity are directly proportional at the same $g$.
Dividing mass by $g$ is not the gravity calculation.

## 6: Using Gravity Formula for Weight

Task: state when $P=gm$ can calculate weight.
Use the textbook's stipulated horizontal support, stationary relative
to Earth. Under those conditions the body acts on the support with a
weight equal in magnitude to its gravity. The definition still
distinguishes the body as the receiver of gravity from the support
or suspension as the receiver of weight.
Do not replace both conditions by "the object is on Earth" or by a
claim that weight and gravity always have the same magnitude.

association and units. These are a coherent application of one conversion
and proportionality rule, not four unrelated answer blanks.

| Original mass | Mass in kilograms | $g=9.8$ result, newtons | $g\approx10$ result, newtons |

Exact decimal arithmetic: $800/1000=0.8$; $1.2\times1000=1200$;

decimal products to integers.

## g2-2: Two Masses to Two Weights

Task: calculate the weights of the 10 kilogram and 200 gram bodies,
using the lesson's horizontal stationary support convention.
First $200$ grams $=0.2$ kilograms. Then:

$$P_1=9.8\times10=98\ \text{牛顿},\qquad
P_2=9.8\times0.2=1.96\ \text{牛顿}.$$

The allowed integer-coefficient results are approximately 100 newtons
and 2 newtons. Both source objects, their units and the weight conditions
must be accounted for. A correct forward weight calculation is
mathematically distinct from recovering mass from a known weight.

## g2-3: Weight to Mass

Task: recover a person's mass from a weight of 800 newtons.
Under the textbook's weight conditions, $P=gm$, so $m=P/g$:

$$m=\frac{800}{9.8}\ \text{千克}
=\frac{4000}{49}\ \text{千克}
=81.632653\ldots\ \text{千克}.$$

If an option requires one decimal place, this is 81.6 kilograms; to
the nearest kilogram it is 82 kilograms. The original source itself
does not specify either rounding rule. With $g\approx10$, the source
also allows an approximate mass of 80 kilograms.
Those different values arise from different stated conventions, not
automatically from flawed mathematics. The essential inference is
division by $g$ and dimensional reduction from newtons to kilograms,
not multiplication by $g$ or relabeling 800 newtons as 800 kilograms.
