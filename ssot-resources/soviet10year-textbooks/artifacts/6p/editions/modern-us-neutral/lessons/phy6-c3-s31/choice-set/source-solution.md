## Source1: Examples of Decreasing Pressure by Increasing Area

Examples include a broader building foundation, long supporting tracks on a vehicle, wide skis or snowshoes supporting a person, and broader wheel contact patches on soft ground. In each comparison keep the supported normal load unchanged and increase the actual area carrying it. Then \(p=F/S\) is smaller.

A larger-looking surface is insufficient if it does not contact/support the load. Merely reducing the load can also reduce pressure, but is not an example of the requested increased-area method. These examples share one mechanism; several materials or devices do not constitute several new laws. The task requires freely producing suitable examples, which recognition alone cannot prove.

## Source2: Broad Rims on Agricultural Machines

For comparable load, a wide rim can provide a broader actual support patch on soil. Distributing the same force over a greater area reduces average pressure and the tendency to sink into soft ground. This is why the width is useful in the lesson's pressure explanation.

The inference concerns effective soil-contact area, not an automatic decrease of machine weight or a universal guarantee against sinking. Soil strength, actual contact and loading still matter. No numerical load or tire contact area is supplied.

## Source3: Sharp Cutting and Piercing Tools

A sharp edge or point concentrates a given normal force on a small contact area. Since \(p=F/S\), even a modest force can give a large local average pressure, assisting cutting or piercing.

This does not mean sharpening must increase the applied force, that pressure and force are the same quantity, or that an ideal zero-area point gives a measured infinite pressure. Actual contact is finite and the material response is more complicated than the elementary average-pressure model.

## Source g2-2: Weighting a Harrow

For the lesson's comparison, keep the effective tooth contact area approximately unchanged initially. Adding weight increases the downward force on the teeth, so \(p=F/S\) increases and the teeth more readily enter hard soil.

The pressure increase here is caused by greater force, not automatically by increased contact area. If the same force were spread over more area, pressure would decrease. As penetration progresses the actual contact can change, so no constant-area exact real-soil time law or guaranteed depth is asserted.

## Source g2-3: Standing versus Running Contact Stage

The source deliberately holds the normal force unchanged and compares the two-foot standing area with the smaller one-foot supporting area in the running contact stage. Therefore \(p_{\rm one}>p_{\rm two}\). In the additional special case of equal effective per-foot area \(s\), the two areas are \(s\) and \(2s\), so the ratio is2.

## Source g3-1: Measure Shoe Support and Estimate Pressures

Measure body mass \(m\) in kg. Measure the actual supporting patches separately for the walking single-foot stage and the stationary two-foot stage, including forefoot and heel where both support, but excluding noncontact gaps. The force model is \(F\approx mg\), with \(g\) in N/kg and the supplied neglect of vertical acceleration.

For a support region with \(n\) complete cells and \(k\) incomplete cells crossed by the boundary, the stated counting rule gives
\[
C\approx n+\frac{k}{2},\qquad S\approx Cc,
\]
where \(c\) is the measured area of one square. Sum distinct support regions without double counting. The half-boundary rule is an estimate, not an exact area theorem.

For the specified workbook grid \(c=\frac14\ {\rm cm}^2\). Since \(1\ {\rm cm}^2=10^{-4}\ {\rm m}^2\),
\[
S\approx\left(n+\frac{k}{2}\right)\frac14\,10^{-4}\ {\rm m}^2.
\]
For a different grid first determine its actual cell area; do not transfer the workbook scale merely because the screenshot displays squares.

Let the resulting areas in square metres be \(S_w\) and \(S_s\), respectively. Then
\[
p_w\approx\frac{mg}{S_w},\qquad
p_s\approx\frac{mg}{S_s},\qquad
\frac{p_w}{p_s}\approx\frac{S_s}{S_w}.
\]
Only if the two measured support configurations satisfy \(S_s=2S_w\) is the pressure ratio2. Do not replace both measured areas with an automatic two-to-one area assumption.

There are no measured personal mass, boundary counts or support-area data in this seed; figure89 is not a personal measurement. Numerical personal answers therefore cannot be produced honestly. Use a stated local \(g\), retaining its units; the seed does not prescribe a numeric value or rounding convention. The available solution is the complete measurement/conversion/estimation method, not fabricated observations.

## Source g3-2: Brick Measurements, Density, Orientations and Pressure

Measure the brick's three perpendicular dimensions \(l,w,h\) in metres. Obtain the density \(\rho\) of the relevant brick material from a density table in kg per cubic metre. The elementary model is a uniform rectangular brick at rest on a horizontal plane, with its weight as normal load.

\[
V=lwh,\qquad m=\rho V,\qquad F=\rho lwh\,g.
\]

The six faces form three equal-area opposing pairs. Define placements by the actual support face rather than an ambiguous name:

| Placement | Supporting face and area | Vertical edge | Average pressure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flat | \(l\times w\), area \(lw\) | \(h\) | \(F/(lw)=\rho gh\) |
| Side | \(l\times h\), area \(lh\) | \(w\) | \(F/(lh)=\rho gw\) |
| Upright | \(w\times h\), area \(wh\) | \(l\) | \(F/(wh)=\rho gl\) |

Moving the same brick between these resting orientations does not change its volume, mass or weight. Pressure changes because the supporting area changes. If the measured dimensions satisfy \(l>w>h\), upright pressure is largest and flat smallest; if their order differs or some are equal, order pressures from the corresponding actual face areas instead.

To draw the three placements, place each stated face on the horizontal baseline and label its two in-plane dimensions and the remaining vertical dimension. That construction specification fixes all three orientations without guessing from a perspective picture. Recognizing this correspondence later will not establish that a learner can produce the three drawings.

The seed supplies no measured brick dimensions, table density or drawing of a particular measured brick. No lookup value, numerical weight/pressure or completed measurement-specific drawing is invented here. If lengths are measured in cm, convert them to m before this SI calculation, and ensure density units are compatible. A total sum of all six face areas is not the supporting area for any one placement.
