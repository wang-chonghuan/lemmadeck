## Source1: An Experiment Demonstrating Gas Pressure on Walls

Original task: How can an experiment demonstrate that gas produces pressure on container walls?

**Setup and operation.** Put a tied, nonleaking balloon containing a small amount of air inside the closed bell jar. It initially remains only weakly inflated, as in figure90(a). The balloon acts as a deformable gas-container wall. Use the evacuation connection to remove air from the bell jar outside the balloon, not from the balloon interior and not by pumping additional gas into it.

**Expected observation.** As the air surrounding the balloon is removed, the balloon expands, approaching figure90(b). This is an expected qualitative observation for the stated setup, not a claimed experiment carried out by the author and not an invented numerical measurement.

**Pressure explanation.** Gas molecules strike both faces of the balloon membrane. Evacuation reduces the outside pressure. Before substantial expansion changes the inside state, the reduction of outside pressure increases the outward pressure imbalance, so the balloon's internal gas can push the deformable wall outward. The response of the wall when its external counterpressure is reduced supplies experimental evidence that the gas inside exerts pressure on that wall.

The tied balloon's molecular total remains unchanged if it does not leak; that does not keep its density or pressure constant while it expands. Volume and density change, and its inside pressure can change too. A vacuum is not a new pulling substance, and the pump does not create inside gas through the tied neck. At a final stationary size, gas-pressure forces and the membrane's elastic forces balance. Stationarity does not by itself imply equal inside/outside gas pressures or absence of either pressure.

Block7 identifies pressure as average wall force per unit area. The source supplies no force/area readings or numerical pressure difference, so the observation is qualitative. A rigid wall's lack of visible motion would not establish zero pressure; the balloon is deliberately deformable.

**Task and diagnostic distinction.** An adequate answer connects apparatus, correctly identified evacuated region, change of wall shape and pressure mechanism. Merely stating that the balloon got larger without explaining which gas acts on which side does not complete the task.

## Source2: Experimental Evidence for Static Isotropy

Original task: From what experimental phenomenon can one see that a gas in static equilibrium produces equal pressure in all directions?

**Setup and observable phenomenon.** Continue the balloon demonstration, using a membrane whose properties are approximately uniform and allowing the gas and balloon to reach a stationary equilibrium without a directional constraint dominating its shape. The enlarged balloon becomes approximately spherical, as in figure90(b), rather than systematically stretching in one preferred direction.

**Inference and its conditions.** In the lesson's small-balloon, approximately uniform-membrane model, this direction-independent equilibrium shape supports the statement that static gas pressure is isotropic. The local definition is: at the same position, a gas in static equilibrium has the same pressure for differently oriented surfaces. It is a statement about pressure, not identical vector-force directions, equal total forces on unequal areas, or equal pressure at every spatial position.

To relate this to forces, compare equal areas under otherwise comparable conditions: their average normal force per unit area has no preferred direction. Wall normals differ, so force vectors need not point the same way. With unequal areas the total force need not be equal even if pressure is equal. A macroscopic gas can be stationary while its molecules continue irregular microscopic motion. The statistically averaged wall impacts explain pressure and its direction independence; individual impact counts during a short snapshot need not be equal.

**Limit of the evidence.** Shape alone in an arbitrarily thick, constrained or nonuniform membrane is not a general proof of pressure isotropy. It is evidence under the given equilibrium and membrane assumptions. Static isotropy at one position does not assert that gravity can never produce pressure variation between different heights. A flowing gas or a deforming, nonequilibrium balloon is not automatically covered by this static statement.

Figure91 can illustrate continuing microscopic motion and wall interactions but cannot replace the requested macroscopic experimental phenomenon. Counting its illustrated arrows on different walls cannot establish equal measured pressures. No time interval, area reading, pressure calibration or statistical sample is supplied by that diagram.

**Task and diagnostic distinction.** An adequate answer names the spherical equilibrium balloon phenomenon and explains why it is relevant under the assumptions. It distinguishes macroscopic equilibrium from molecular stillness, pressure from force, and direction at a fixed position from spatial uniformity.
