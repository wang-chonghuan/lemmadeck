## Original Definitions That Control the Solutions

These conventions are binding; importing the common modern strict meaning
of parallel would change this chapter's answers.

1. Original n2: a line intersects a plane if their intersection is exactly
   one point. Original n3 likewise uses exactly one common point for
   intersecting lines.
2. Original n3-n4: lines are parallel if coplanar and disjoint **or
   coincident**. There is exactly one parallel through any point, including
   the given line when the point lies on it.
3. Original n6: skew means neither intersecting nor parallel, equivalently
   noncoplanar. Its criterion is: one line lies in a plane and the other
   intersects that plane at a point outside the first line.
4. Original n7: line-plane parallel includes both an empty intersection
   and containment. A direction parallel to the plane is therefore parallel
   whether its line is outside or inside the plane.
5. Original n10-n11: plane-plane parallel includes disjoint planes and
   coincident planes. Their direction planes are the same. The parallel
   plane through a given point is unique, and equals the given plane if
   the point belongs to it.
6. Original n5: a polyhedron-plane intersection is a section when it is
   a polygon. A point or segment contact is not to be silently promoted to
   a polygon. The target lesson's note says the requested constructions
   mean constructions of intuitive/projection drawings.
7. Original n12: parallel projection has one fixed direction transverse
   to the target plane; every point has a unique projection. Its
   noncollapsed-line properties are explicitly qualified by the source.
   Collapsed lines and segments must still be considered in exercise 149.
8. Original n13: parallelism and affine ratios are represented faithfully
   in the drawing, but arbitrary angles and lengths need not be. No
   equality of nonparallel lengths may be inferred from the raster.

## Reusable Independent Section Construction

This is mathematical construction detail shared by 134, 145 and 147, not a
tool, pipeline, test oracle or generated figure.

For a nondegenerate tetrahedron use barycentric coordinates
`X = lambda_A A + lambda_B B + lambda_C C + lambda_D D`,
with sum of lambdas 1. Membership in the solid means all lambdas are
nonnegative. Face coordinates and ratios can be constructed in its
intuitive drawing by parallels in the face triangles.

An affine plane has equation
`h_A lambda_A + h_B lambda_B + h_C lambda_C + h_D lambda_D = 0`,
where the four h values are its signed vertex values, up to common nonzero
scale. For noncollinear specified points M,N,P, impose this equation at
each: three independent homogeneous equations give one nonzero h vector
up to scale. That determines the plane without a general-position guess.

For every one of the six edges UV:

- Opposite nonzero signs: construct the unique point
  `X = (1-t)U + t V`, `t = h_U/(h_U-h_V)`, with `0<t<1`.
- Exactly one zero: include that endpoint once.
- Both zero: include the entire edge.
- Same nonzero sign: no point of the closed edge is cut.

Clip each face to its plane trace and join boundary points on their actual
common faces. Merge repeated vertices and collinear adjoining pieces; do
not invent a side at a vertex or double-count an edge lying in the plane.
This constructs the intersection, including endpoint and coincident-face
branches. A noncollinear M,N,P inside the solid ensure positive section
area. A tetrahedron's polygonal section has three or four sides.

For a parallelepiped use its affine cube coordinates:
`A=(0,0,0), B=(1,0,0), D=(0,1,0), A_1=(0,0,1)`,
`C=(1,1,0), B_1=(1,0,1), D_1=(0,1,1), C_1=(1,1,1)`.
The solid is `0<=x,y,z<=1`; coordinates refer to the independent edge
vectors, not an assumption of right angles. Solve
`h(x,y,z)=a x+b y+c z-d=0` at M,N,P, and apply the identical interpolation
rule to all twelve edges and all six faces. Affine ratios used to place
these cut points are preserved by the original projection construction.
No numerical coordinate assumption is needed.

Affine rank is checked before naming `(MNP)`:

- Rank 2: a unique plane, and a polygonal section if these three points
  lie in the solid.
- Rank 1: a pencil of planes through their line, not one specified plane.
  The question then specifies a family, not a unique section.
- Rank 0: all three points coincide; every plane through the point is a
  candidate. Polygonal, segment, or point contacts depend on that choice.

For any plane and a convex six-faced solid, each face can supply at most
one actual side. Polygonal sections therefore have at most six sides.
These sign rules exhaust possible positions; they are not an instruction
to skip exceptional cases.

## 121

Source request (`p0049#6`): Given n points, `n>=4`, no four in one plane,
how many different planes can be made through three of them?

Independent solution: **n(n-1)(n-2)/6**.

No three points can be collinear: otherwise those three together with any
fourth point belong to a plane through the line and the fourth point
(or any plane through the line if the fourth is also on it). This
contradicts the premise. Each triple therefore determines one plane.
Two different triples cannot determine the same plane: their union has
at least four points. Thus the planes correspond bijectively to
three-element subsets, giving `binomial(n,3)`.

Determination: unique count. No exceptional triple allowed by the premise.

## 123

Source request (`p0049#8`, `p0050#1`): If a line has common points with

5) six edges of a parallelepiped, can it lie in a plane containing a face?

Answers in order: **no; yes; yes; yes; yes**.

Count edges, not distinct contact points. Contact with a vertex counts
each of its three incident edges. By an invertible affine map it suffices
to inspect the cube `[0,1]^3`, with the line in the face plane `z=0`.
No upper edge meets this plane. Among remaining edges, the line meets
bottom-face edges and also a vertical edge exactly when it passes through
that bottom vertex.

Exhaustive possibilities for a line in that face plane are:

- Missing the square: 0 edges.
- Crossing two relative-interior side points: 2 edges.
- Touching one vertex only: 3 edges.
- Passing through one vertex and another side's interior: 4 edges.
- Coinciding with a square side: that side, its two neighboring bottom
  sides and the two endpoint vertical edges, or 5 edges.
- Passing through two opposite vertices: 6 edges.

There is no 1-edge case. A line touching the square at a nonvertex
interior side point must either enter the square or coincide with that
side; neither gives exactly one counted edge.

3. `x+y=0`: only the vertex A as a point, but three edges.
4. `y=x/2`: A and the interior of the opposite side at `(1,1/2,0)`,

5. `y=x`: A and C, giving six different incident edges.

The unasked 5-edge case is recorded to make the exclusion of 1 exhaustive.

## 124

Source request (`p0050#2`): Is the sufficient skew-line condition stated
in section 6 also necessary?

**Yes, with the existential quantifier appropriate to the criterion.**
If a,b are skew, choose any B on b. Then B is outside a. The unique plane
`Pi=(a,B)` contains a. It cannot contain b, since that would make a,b
coplanar. Hence `b cap Pi={B}` and B is outside a. This is exactly the
criterion, proving necessity as well as its original sufficiency.

It is not true that *every* arbitrarily preselected plane containing a
must meet b. For instance `a={(s,0,0)}`, `b={(0,t,1)}` are skew;
the plane `z=0` contains a and misses b, while the plane `y=0` contains a
and meets b at `(0,0,1)`. That quantifier distinction does not invalidate
the necessary-and-sufficient existential condition.

## 126

Source request (`p0050#4`, fig-54): Two skew lines m,n, and P on neither.
Construct a line through P meeting both.

There are **one-solution and no-solution cases**; P being off both lines
does not ensure existence.

Construct `Pi=(P,m)`. A required transversal must be in Pi, because
it contains P and its intersection point with m. The line n cannot be
contained in Pi, since m,n are skew.

1. If n misses Pi, there is no solution.
2. Otherwise construct `N=n cap Pi`, a unique point. Since P is not on n,
   N differs from P. The only candidate is `q=(PN)`.
3. In Pi, if q is parallel to m, it misses m and there is no solution.
   If not parallel, construct `M=q cap m`; q is the unique solution.
   It cannot coincide with m because P is not on m.

An exhaustive model makes the existence restrictions transparent.
Take affine coordinates
`m={(s,0,0)}`, `n={(0,t,1)}`, `P=(x,y,z)`.
For intersections `(s,0,0)` and `(0,t,1)` collinear with P, necessarily
`x=(1-z)s`, `y=z t`. Thus:

- `z notin {0,1}`: the unique intersections are
  `s=x/(1-z)` and `t=y/z`.
- `z=0`, with P off m, so `y!=0`: no solution.
- `z=1`, with P off n, so `x!=0`: no solution.

Geometrically the excluded planes are the plane through m parallel to
the direction of n, and the plane through n parallel to the direction
of m. The latter is the q-parallel-m failure above.

Witnesses: `P=(1,1,1/2)` succeeds; `P=(0,1,0)` and `P=(1,0,1)` fail
in the two respective ways. The depicted P between M,N is one admissible

## 128

Source request (`p0050#8`): a intersects alpha. Can a line parallel to a
be constructed in alpha?

**No.** "Intersects" means one point, so a has a direction transverse
to alpha. Every line in alpha has a direction in alpha. A parallel to a
has a's transverse direction and cannot lie in alpha.

Equivalently, if a were parallel to a line in alpha, original n7's
criterion would imply a parallel to alpha, contrary to the one-point
intersection. Inclusive parallelism does not change this answer.

## 129

3. Through F, in BCD, draw the parallel to CD; it cuts BD at G.
4. Join H,G. The required section is EFGH, in that cyclic order.

Proof and existence: write
`M=mu_A A+mu_B B+mu_C C`, with all three mu positive and sum 1, and
`s=mu_A+mu_B`, so `0<s<1`. The unique plane through M with the independent
directions AB and CD has equation `lambda_A+lambda_B=s`.
Its edge intersections are

`E=s A+(1-s)C`, `F=s B+(1-s)C`,
`G=s B+(1-s)D`, `H=s A+(1-s)D`.

All four are relative-interior edge points. They give
`EF=HG=s(B-A)` and `EH=FG=(1-s)(D-C)` as vectors.
The section is a nondegenerate parallelogram, not necessarily a rectangle.
The other two edges AB and CD have constant vertex values 1 and 0 and
are not met. Thus these four points exhaust the boundary.

Uniqueness: AB and CD are skew, so their directions are independent and
determine one direction plane. Its translate through M is unique.
The premise that M is in the face interior excludes endpoint degeneracy.

Answers: **yes; yes; no; yes; no**.

Use a unit cube for existence witnesses:

   `(t,0,0)`, `(0,t,0)`, `(0,0,t)`. Each side has length `t sqrt(2)`,

2. `z=1/2` gives a square of side 1.
3. A five-sided cube section uses five different faces. Among five
   cube faces, an opposite pair must occur. Their traces in the

   180 degrees, are all distinct. Therefore it cannot be such a section.

5. A cube has six faces and a section has at most one actual side per
   face, so it cannot have eight sides.

`M=(1-m)B+m C`,
`P=(1-p)A+p B`,
`Q=(1-q)C+q D`,

`m lambda_B-(1-m)lambda_C=0`.

`u=m p>=0` and `-v=-(1-m)(1-q)<=0`.

- If `u+v>0`, construct the division point
  `T=(v P+u Q)/(u+v)` on the closed segment PQ.
  It is the unique zero of that affine equation on the supporting
  line. The internal ratio is `PT:TQ=u:v`, allowing an endpoint
  when either numerator is zero.
- If `u=v=0`, the entire line PQ lies in Pi. Its intersection with
  Pi is the line, not a unique point.
