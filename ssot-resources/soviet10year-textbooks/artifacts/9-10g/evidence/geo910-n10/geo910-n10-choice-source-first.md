## Stage1 definition and proof context

The section is “§ 10. 两个平面的相关位置. 平面平行的判定”.
Distinct planes either meet in a complete line or have no common point;
coincident planes are the third relationship. The book explicitly combines
disjointness and coincidence as plane parallelism. Line-plane parallelism from
the established course convention similarly includes containment or no common
point; it does not mean only disjointness.

Theorem6: two intersecting lines in one plane, respectively parallel to two
lines of another plane, imply parallel planes. The intersecting-line condition
is substantive. Coincident planes are explicitly included and illustrated.

For the initial different-plane construction, a,b intersect in the lower
plane; through an external point draw respectively parallel a1,b1 and their
unique upper plane. An assumed common M gives an intersection line c.
Theorem4 would make c parallel to BOTH intersecting a,b, contradicting
uniqueness of a parallel direction through their common point. The assumed
common M in this proof is a contradiction hypothesis, not a valid additional
given of a realizable parallel-plane configuration.

The faithful lesson currently names the external point beta_1 and the
lower intersection point beta, whereas the directly viewed source figure uses
B_1 and B. The symbolic proof currently writes alpha cap beta = uppercase C,

### 77: preserve the unmarked first condition and the printed2)

Full current faithful text, including the absent1) marker, is preserved:

> $77^{\bullet}$. 如果已知：某一条直线 $a$ 在平面 $\alpha$ 内，但不在平面$\beta$ 内；2) 平面 $\alpha$ 内的任意一条直线都不在平面 $\beta$ 内，平面 $\alpha$ 和 $\beta$ 的位置关系怎样？

**Unmarked first condition.** Alpha and beta cannot coincide, because their
line sets would then be identical. They may intersect in a line OR be
disjoint parallel planes. A line “not in beta” means not contained in beta,
not necessarily disjoint from beta. For examples, alpha z=0 with beta z=1
is disjoint; alpha z=0 with beta y=0 intersects, and the line
{(t,1,0)} belongs to alpha but not beta.

**Printed2).** They must be disjoint. If coincident, every alpha line lies
in beta. If intersecting, the complete common line belongs to both, violating
the universal condition. Therefore no common point is possible.
Calling this merely “parallel” without excluding coincidence loses the
distinction requested under the book's broad definition.

The original first condition is neither dropped nor assigned an invented
source marker. Analytical headings above distinguish its two clause tasks;
they do not change the source text.

### 78: both assertions are false without two intersecting directions

Take alpha: z=0 and beta: y=0. These planes intersect in the x-axis.
In alpha take a={(t,1,0)}, and in beta take a1={(t,0,1)}.
They are distinct parallel lines, yet the planes are not parallel.
This refutes1), even without using the allowed coincident-line branch.

For2), also take b={(t,2,0)} in alpha and b1={(t,0,2)} in beta.
Then a parallel a1 and b parallel b1, with all four lines distinct.
Alpha and beta still intersect. The two alpha lines share only a direction,
not an intersection. Theorem6's missing intersecting-line hypothesis is what
prevents this counterexample. Do not silently add that hypothesis to78.

### 81: quadrilateral yes; pentagon and every n>5 no in general

Let pi be the polygon's plane, d the specified diagonal's supporting line,
and s the specified side's supporting line. Under the broad course definition,
both lines parallel alpha means their directions lie in alpha's direction
plane. If d,s have independent directions, those directions span pi, so
pi parallel alpha. If they have the same direction, the condition determines
only one direction and need not imply parallel planes.

**Quadrilateral.** Every side shares an endpoint with either diagonal.
In a genuine nondegenerate quadrilateral that side and diagonal are distinct
intersecting lines; coincidence would make a consecutive vertex triple
collinear, collapsing a polygon corner. Thus the two directions are independent
and the assertion is true. Convexity or regularity is not required.
This uses the ordinary nondegenerate polygon meaning, not a new condition that
every three vertices of an arbitrary polygon are noncollinear. If a permitted
course definition explicitly admitted collapsed consecutive corners, that
definition would need to be bound before a later key; none is invented here.

**Pentagon counterexample, already convex.** In pi: z=0 choose, in cyclic order:

`A=(-2,0,0), B=(0,-2,0), C=(2,0,0), D=(1,1,0), E=(-1,1,0)`.

All consecutive turns are positive, so this is a proper convex pentagon.
Diagonal AC and side DE are distinct x-direction lines. Let alpha be y=2.
Both supporting lines are disjoint from alpha and therefore parallel to it,
even under a stricter disjoint-only line-plane definition. But pi and alpha
intersect in {(t,2,0)}. The pentagon assertion is false.

**Counterexample for each integer n>5.** Retain D,E and endpoints A,C.
Replace the A-B-C lower chain with n-2 distinct vertices, in increasing
x order, on `y=x^2/2-2, z=0`, with x=-2,0,2 included. This adds exactly
n-5 vertices without placing consecutive vertices on one straight line.
The resulting n-gon is proper and convex. AC remains a diagonal and DE
remains a side; both retain the x direction and are disjoint from y=2.
Its plane z=0 still intersects y=2. Thus the assertion fails for EVERY n>5,
not merely for a special hexagon or an assumed regular polygon.

Do not add “the side and diagonal intersect”, “are not parallel”, “the
polygon is regular”, or “the plane is distinct from alpha” to the written
source. The convex counterexamples require none of those extra restrictions.

### 82: midpoint plane of a general tetrahedron

In face DAB, the midpoint theorem gives MN parallel AB.
In face DAC it gives MP parallel AC. MN,MP intersect at M; AB,AC intersect
at A. Theorem6 yields plane MNP parallel plane ABC.

The tetrahedron is nondegenerate, so M,N,P are noncollinear. With D as origin
and A=u,B=v,C=w an independent basis, the midpoint plane has coefficient
sum1/2, while ABC has coefficient sum1. The planes are distinct/disjoint,
not just a coincident case. No regular-tetrahedron assumption is supplied.

### 83: central inversion of the two triples

Set O=0 and u=OA1, v=OB1, w=OC1. The three concurrent lines are not all
coplanar, so these three nonzero vectors are independent. Opposite sides and
the three pairwise equal-distance givens imply
`A2=-u, B2=-v, C2=-w`.

Therefore A2B2 has the negative direction of A1B1, and A2C2 the negative
direction of A1C1. Each triple is noncollinear; its two displayed edges
intersect. Theorem6 gives the required parallel planes.

More fully, in the independent u,v,w basis the first plane has coefficient
sum1 and the second sum-1, so they are distinct. No equality BETWEEN the
three line-specific radii was assumed: only OA1=OA2, OB1=OB2, OC1=OC2.
Central symmetry/inversion is an own proof, not a new source drawing.
