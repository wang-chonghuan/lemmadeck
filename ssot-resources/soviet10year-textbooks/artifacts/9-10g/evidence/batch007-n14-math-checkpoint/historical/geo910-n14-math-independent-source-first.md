## 4. Independently Derived Mathematical Toolkit

### P: Parallel Projection and Every Rank Case

Let target plane be

`alpha = {x in U : n dot x = c}`, with `n != 0`.

Let `d != 0` represent the direction of original `l`. The projection is defined for every point precisely when

`n dot d != 0`.

Indeed the carrier through `x` consists of `x + t d`. Solving `n dot (x + t d) = c` gives the unique parameter `t = (c - n dot x)/(n dot d)`, hence

`P(x) = x - ((n dot x - c)/(n dot d)) d`.

This is parallel projection, not necessarily perpendicular projection. If `n dot d = 0`, an off-plane carrier never intersects the target, whereas an on-plane carrier lies in the target and has infinitely many intersections. Thus no total, uniquely defined projection of `U` results in that case. "Intersecting" in the original projection construction must mean a single transverse intersection, not containment.

Direct consequences, proved by this formula:

1. `P(x)` belongs to `alpha`; for `x in alpha`, `P(x) = x`. Consequently `P(U) = alpha`.
2. `P(x + t d) = P(x)` for all real `t`.
3. Conversely `P(x) = P(y)` implies `x-y` is a scalar multiple of `d`. Each fiber is exactly one entire line parallel to `l`.
4. The linear difference map `Q(v) = v - ((n dot v)/(n dot d)) d` has kernel `span(d)` and image the two-dimensional direction space of `alpha`; its rank is two.
5. For an affine carrier flat `H = a + V` of dimension `k`, the restricted rank is `k - dim(V intersect span(d))`. Its kernel dimension is zero or one, not an arbitrarily imposed "generic position."

Complete flat-rank inventory:

| Original affine hull | Condition | Restricted rank and image |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point | Any point | Rank 0, one image point |
| Line | Its direction parallel to `d`, including coincidence with a carrier | Rank 0, point |
| Line | Its direction not parallel to `d` | Rank 1, entire line |
| Plane | `d` lies in its direction space, i.e. inclusive parallelity to `l` | Rank 1, entire line |
| Plane | `d` not in its direction space | Rank 2, the whole target plane |
| Space | Always, for valid projection | Rank 2, the whole target plane |

A genuine planar triangle or trapezoid cannot have rank-zero image: its direction space is two-dimensional and cannot lie inside a one-dimensional kernel. A genuine full-dimensional tetrahedron or parallelepiped cannot project to a segment or point under a valid parallel projection.

For finite vertices `v_i` and nonnegative weights `lambda_i` with sum one,

`P(sum lambda_i v_i) = sum lambda_i P(v_i)`.

This follows by substituting the formula, including its constant term. Thus a filled convex polytope projects exactly to the convex hull of its projected vertices; conversely each convex combination downstairs has its same-weight preimage upstairs.

For a compact convex three-dimensional solid, projecting only its entire boundary gives the same filled planar shadow: the carrier over any shadow point intersects the solid in a nonempty compact interval or point, and its endpoint(s) belong to the boundary. A wireframe consisting only of edges is different and must not silently be equated with the filled shadow. For planar triangles/trapezoids, boundary-only rank-two images remain polygon boundaries, while rank-one boundary projections cover the whole shadow segment.

### C: Central Symmetry, Including the Center

Use analytic alias `C_O`, without changing the source's literal `$Z_0$`. For center coordinate `o`,

`C_O(x) = 2o - x`.

For `x != o`, this is the unique point for which `o` is the midpoint of the segment joining `x` and its image: it is on the same line, on the opposite side of `o`, and equally distant from `o`. For `x = o`, the formula fixes `o`, agreeing with the source's explicit special case.

Uniqueness and injectivity: if `2o-x = 2o-y`, then `x=y`. Surjectivity: for any `y in U`, take `x = 2o-y`; then `C_O(x)=y`. These are separately proved properties, not a definition by the word "bijection."

Involution:

`C_O(C_O(x)) = 2o - (2o-x) = x` for every `x in U`.

Affine combinations are preserved, with coefficients summing to one. Lines, segments, and planes therefore map to lines, segments, and planes, respectively. Distances are preserved because image differences are negatives of original differences. None of these facts is inferred from fig-59's apparent slope or length.

### T: Literal Transformation Definition, Inverses, and Composition

The literal source definition is a total map `f: U -> U` with distinct images for any two distinct points. This imposes injectivity. It does **not** explicitly impose `f(U)=U`. The phrase "到它自身" identifies the codomain; it cannot silently be substituted for "onto," since the same lesson distinguishes image containment from image equality.

Independent counterexample to automatic surjectivity: choose coordinates in space and define

`h(t) = t` if `t < 0`, and `h(t) = t+1` if `t >= 0`;

`f(x,y,z) = (h(x),y,z)`.

On either half-line `h` is injective, and their images `(-infinity,0)` and `[1,infinity)` are disjoint. Thus `f` is total and injective, satisfying the printed transformation definition. However no point with first coordinate in `[0,1)` has a preimage. This example requires no continuity, distance preservation, or other unstated restriction.

Swapping the graph's pairs yields a unique inverse

`f^{-1}: f(U) -> U`.

For the example its first-coordinate inverse is `s` when `s<0`, and `s-1` when `s>=1`; it is undefined on the missing slab. Injectivity ensures uniqueness of an existing preimage, not existence at every point of `U`.

A global two-sided inverse `g: U -> U` exists if and only if `f` is also onto:

- Necessity: `f(g(y))=y` for all `y in U` forces every point into `f(U)`.
- Sufficiency: totality, injectivity, and onto together give one preimage for each `y`; the swapped graph is then a total injective self-map and both identity equations hold.
- One cannot extend the image-domain inverse to an injective self-map on missing points while keeping all existing inverse values: those existing values already exhaust `U`, leaving no unused output for any additional point.

For every printed transformation the image-domain inverse always gives

`f^{-1} after f = E_U`.

The other order gives `f after f^{-1} = E_{f(U)}`, only on the inverse's domain. Calling the inverse another **spatial transformation** on all `U` requires the extra onto premise. This is an actual definitional gap in body object B20, not a source text correction.

Composition: if `f_1,f_2: U -> U` are total and injective, then `f_2(f_1(x))` exists for every `x`, and equality of two such images implies equality first of the `f_1` images and then of the original points. Thus `f_2 circ f_1` is a transformation under the literal definition, without requiring onto. The right-hand map acts first.

For the composite's image-domain inverse, the correct order is

`(f_2 circ f_1)^{-1} = f_1^{-1} circ f_2^{-1}`,

on domain `f_2(f_1(U))`. There `f_2^{-1}` returns a point in `f_1(U)`, on which `f_1^{-1}` is defined. If both maps are onto, this is a global identity of self-maps on `U`.

Order need not commute. Independent midpoint-based example: for distinct centers with coordinates `a,b`,

`C_B(C_A(x)) = x+2(b-a)`,

whereas `C_A(C_B(x)) = x+2(a-b)`.

They differ when `a != b`. This is a derived order check, not a premise taken from a later translation lesson. Same-center composition is treated in task 157.

### Task 153, p0057#7

Original:

> 153°. 在平行投影下，下列图形的象是什么图形？1) 三角形；2) 梯形；3) 四面体；4) 平行六面体.

Results describe the shape type; no angles, arbitrary length ratios between nonparallel directions, or cube metrics are retained by assumption.

#### 153(1): Triangle

Let `H=aff(A,B,C)` for noncollinear vertices.

- If `l` is not inclusively parallel to `H`, rank is two. The image is a nondegenerate triangle with vertices `P(A),P(B),P(C)`, by injectivity on `H` and preservation of affine combinations.
- If `l` is inclusively parallel to `H`, rank is one. The image is a nonzero segment, the convex hull of the three collinear vertex images. Some vertex images may coincide when their connecting edge is parallel to `l`; they need not coincide, and the middle image is not necessarily the image of the vertex that looks central in a sketch.
- Rank zero is impossible for a genuine triangle. Point images become possible only if one separately admits a degenerate collinear/point "triangle" and its affine hull is in a fiber; that is not the ordinary source figure.

Both filled-triangle and triangle-boundary interpretations give the same shape names; the rank-two image has the corresponding filled/boundary convention. A connected boundary under rank one projects onto the whole interval between its extreme images.

#### 153(2): Trapezoid

Let `H` be its plane.

- If `l` is not inclusively parallel to `H`, rank two gives an invertible affine map on that plane. The image is a nondegenerate trapezoid. The designated bases remain parallel, and their length ratio is preserved because both difference vectors receive the same directional scale. Nonparallel legs remain nonparallel: proportional projected directions would imply proportional original directions by injectivity of `Q` on `H`.
- Consequently an exact-one-pair trapezoid cannot turn into a parallelogram in rank two. If the adopted definition includes parallelograms, a parallelogram input remains a parallelogram. No choice of that naming convention is forced here.
- If `l` is inclusively parallel to `H`, rank one gives a nonzero segment, the convex hull of the four collinear vertex images. Edges parallel to `l` collapse individually. The resulting extremes need not be the images of the same two vertices for every direction.
- A point image is impossible for an ordinary nondegenerate trapezoid. Right/isosceles properties are not in general preserved under rank two; no perpendicular projection is assumed.

Again a full planar polygon and its boundary must retain their respective rank-two point-set convention; both rank-one shadows are the complete segment.

#### 153(3): Tetrahedron

For a genuine tetrahedron `T=conv(A,B,C,D)`, its affine hull is `U`. Therefore the projected vertices have affine hull of dimension two: if they were all collinear, their hull would contradict `P(U)=alpha`. The shadow is their filled convex hull and has:

- Four distinct extreme vertices: a convex quadrilateral.
- Three extreme vertices: a triangle. The fourth projected vertex can lie strictly inside it, on a side, or coincide with another projected vertex.

There cannot be five or more boundary vertices, since only four generating vertices are present. Nor can a genuine tetrahedron give a segment or point under a valid P. A face can collapse to a segment when its plane contains direction `d`; an edge can collapse to a point when parallel to `d`; these local collapses do not lower the entire tetrahedron shadow below dimension two.

Independent existence examples use `alpha: z=0`, `d=(0,0,1)`, solely as convenient admissible coordinates, not as an assumed perpendicularity in the original task:

1. `A=(0,0,0)`, `B=(1,0,0)`, `C=(1,1,0)`, `D=(0,1,1)` are affinely independent; their projections are the four square corners, hence a quadrilateral shadow. "Square" belongs only to this constructed example.
2. `A=(0,0,0)`, `B=(1,0,0)`, `C=(0,1,0)`, `D=(1/4,1/4,1)` give a triangle shadow with `P(D)` interior.
3. Replacing the last `D` by `(0,0,1)` gives `P(D)=P(A)` and still a triangular shadow.
4. `A=(0,0,0)`, `B=(1,0,0)`, `C=(1,0,1)`, `D=(0,1,0)` give a collapsed `ABC` face but still a triangular shadow. The three projected face vertices are collinear, and the fourth is off that line.

For the entire tetrahedral surface, the shadow is the same filled triangle/quadrilateral by the fiber-endpoint proof in P. For **only the wireframe**, the exact image is the union of all six projected edge segments; in the quadrilateral case this includes its boundary and both diagonals, but not the whole interior. The source sketch does not authorize treating that wireframe interpretation as the only one.

The four-corner example in B07/I58 is one valid branch, not the exhaustive answer to 153(3).

#### 153(4): Parallelepiped

No cube or rectangular-box restriction. By the original definition, write its solid point set as

`K = {a + s u + t v + r w : 0<=s,t,r<=1}`,

where `u,v,w` are linearly independent edge vectors. This coordinate description follows from the parallelogram base and equal parallel lateral edges.

Let `p=Q(u)`, `q=Q(v)`, `r'=Q(w)`. Its shadow is

`P(a) + [0,p] + [0,q] + [0,r']`.

The three projected generators span the two-dimensional target direction space, because `u,v,w` span space and `Q` has rank two. Thus:

- If one generator is zero, the other two are independent, and the image is a parallelogram.
- If all are nonzero but two are parallel, combine their intervals, allowing either same or opposite orientations. Together they form one translated interval in that direction. Adding the third independent interval yields a parallelogram.
- If all three are nonzero and pairwise nonparallel, the image is a centrally symmetric convex hexagon.

Why exactly six in the last branch: for a normal not perpendicular to any generator, the supporting vertex chooses each interval's endpoint according to the sign of its scalar product with that normal. On rotating the normal once around the target plane, its sign changes at two distinct directions for each of the three nonparallel generators. Each change contributes a nonzero edge parallel to that generator, giving exactly six edges/vertices. With only two distinct nonzero generator directions, there are four such changes/edges.

An independent symmetry proof is obtained by replacing `(s,t,r)` with `(1-s,1-t,1-r)`: the shadow's center is `P(a)+(p+q+r')/2`. Therefore no genuine five-sided or triangular filled shadow occurs. Segment/point images are impossible for a genuine three-dimensional parallelepiped.

Examples with `z=0`, `d=(0,0,1)`:

1. `u=(1,0,0)`, `v=(0,1,0)`, `w=(0,0,1)` give the parallelogram branch by a collapsed generator.
2. `u=(1,0,0)`, `v=(0,1,0)`, `w=(2,0,1)` give a parallelogram with a nonzero projected parallel pair, without a collapsed generator.
3. `u=(1,0,0)`, `v=(0,1,0)`, `w=(1,1,1)` give the hexagon with successive boundary vertices `(0,0)`, `(1,0)`, `(2,1)`, `(2,2)`, `(1,2)`, `(0,1)`.

A face shadow may be a parallelogram or segment; an edge may collapse. The whole surface gives the same filled shadow as the solid. An edge-only input instead gives the union of its 12 projected edges, not generally the filled region. If a degenerate coplanar "parallelepiped" were permitted, use the rank table and convex hull anew; such inputs are not the ordinary source's six-faced three-dimensional object.
