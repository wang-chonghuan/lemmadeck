## Author Derivation: Projection and Image

Use affine Euclidean space S with translation vector space V of dimension 3.
For computation only, let

`alpha = {x : n.x = c}`, `n != 0`, direction `L = span(d)`, `d != 0`.

The original unique-intersection assumption is exactly `n.d != 0`. Solving
`n.(x + t d) = c` gives the unique value
`t = (c - n.x)/(n.d)`, so

`P(x) = x - ((n.x - c)/(n.d)) d`.

This does NOT require `d` to be a plane normal. If `n.d = 0`, a projector
through a point off alpha misses alpha, and a projector lying in alpha has
infinitely many intersection points. This is failure of the definition, not
an additional valid "point image" case.

Writing `P(x)=T x + b`, its linear part has
`ker(T)=L`, `im(T)=direction(alpha)`, and `rank(T)=2`.
Proof: `T d=0`; `T v=v` for `n.v=0`; if `T v=0`, the displayed formula makes
v a multiple of d. Therefore

`P(x)=P(y) <=> x-y belongs to L`.

Every fibre is a full line parallel to d. Points of alpha are fixed, alpha
is the entire image of S, and `P(P(x))=P(x)`. Thus P is surjective ONTO alpha
but not injective on S; as an S-to-S map it also is not surjective onto S.

For an affine subspace `Q=a+W`,

`dim P(Q) = dim W - dim(W intersect L)`.

For a full-dimensional polygon inside its supporting plane beta:

- If `d` is not in `direction(beta)`, rank 2: the restriction is an affine
  bijection beta -> alpha. Vertices stay distinct and noncollinear. If
  beta=alpha, this restriction is simply the identity.
- If `d` is in `direction(beta)`, rank 1: the image of the polygon is a
  NONZERO segment. A genuine two-dimensional polygon cannot collapse to a
  point because a one-dimensional kernel cannot contain its whole plane.

This handles the original inclusive phrase "plane ABC is not parallel to l":
the excluded rank-1 case includes BOTH a direction line contained in that
plane and an external line having that plane's direction.

Affine maps commute with convex combinations. For finite vertices v_i,

`P(conv{v_i}) = conv{P(v_i)}`.

Both inclusions follow by applying P to each convex combination; conversely
use the same coefficients on the original vertices. A segment maps to the
segment between its endpoint images, possibly a point. A rank-1 polygon
maps onto the interval between the minimum and maximum vertex values of
its nonzero projected coordinate. Its connected boundary also covers that
interval. Under rank 2, however, a polygon's boundary is a boundary, not
the filled region.

For a compact, convex, three-dimensional solid K, the projection of its
SURFACE equals the projection of the whole solid. Given y in P(K), intersect
K with `y+L`. This is a nonempty compact segment or a single point. An
endpoint lies on the boundary (an interior point would allow extension
along L); it projects to y. This proves both tetrahedron and parallelepiped
surface claims without borrowing an informal worked narrative.

An EDGE FRAME projects instead to the union of its projected edge segments.
Its convex hull has the same silhouette as the whole solid, but the union
is not generally the filled silhouette. Do not confuse an ordered
quadrilateral through vertex labels, a wireframe diagram, and a convex
set-image.

Both ranks occur: choose d outside the triangle plane for rank 2; choose

### 153(2): Trapezoid

Let the trapezoid be a nondegenerate convex planar quadrilateral with its
original parallel base pair.

- Rank 2: a trapezoid. The affine bijection preserves both parallelism and
  nonparallelism, so a proper trapezoid does NOT become a parallelogram.
  If the original convention admits parallelograms as trapezoids, that
  subclass stays a parallelogram; the convention is not tightened here.
- Rank 1: a nonzero segment. Its ends are the extreme projected vertices;
  two, three, or four distinct collinear vertex images are possible.
  Base-direction projection collapses each base separately to a point,
  giving two distinct endpoint images. No plane-wide point collapse occurs.
- In rank 2 the base length ratio is preserved: both base vectors have the
  same linear scale factor in their common direction. This assertion must
  not be used when both projected bases have zero length, producing 0/0.

The rank-1 subcases are genuine: in coordinates on the original plane take
vertices (0,0),(3,0),(2,1),(0,1). Projected scalar coordinates y give two
values; x gives three; x+2y gives four. Each nonzero functional can be
realized as the restriction of a valid spatial P by taking its one-dimensional
kernel for d and choosing a transverse target plane.

### 153(3): Tetrahedron

For a NONDEGENERATE tetrahedron K=conv{A,B,C,D}, the three-dimensional
affine span maps with rank 2. Therefore all projected vertices cannot be
collinear; neither a segment nor a point is possible.

Its whole-solid or whole-surface image is the convex hull of four projected
points. Exactly these two shape classes occur:

- Convex quadrilateral: four projected vertices are distinct and each is
  extreme. This includes the displayed F58 example, whose lower perimeter
  is A_1-B_1-C_1-D_1-A_1.
- Triangle: three extreme projected vertices; the fourth projected vertex
  can lie strictly inside, on an edge, or coincide with one of the three.
  Three projected vertices can be collinear provided the fourth is off
  that line. One coincident pair is possible; two coincident pairs or three
  coincident vertices would force the original affine span to have dimension
  at most 2, so are impossible for a tetrahedron.

If all four projections are distinct and no three are collinear, the
triangle case means one lies inside the other three. Drawing a concave or
self-crossing ordered A_1B_1C_1D_1 is not a proof of a concave set-image:
the convex hull is still convex.

Existence proof for ALL the stated configurations: pick any four planar
points p0,p1,p2,p3 spanning a plane. The 2 x 3 matrix of differences
`(p1-p0,p2-p0,p3-p0)` has rank 2, and hence a one-dimensional kernel
spanned by nonzero r. Choose three heights h with `r.h != 0`. Lifting
`pi` to `pi + hi d` (h0=0) makes the three spatial differences independent:
a relation would have coefficients proportional to r and a zero height
sum, contradicting `r.h != 0`. These lifts form a nondegenerate tetrahedron
with exactly the chosen projections. This covers quadrilateral, interior
point, edge point, and one-coincidence cases without imposing a cube or
metric projection direction.

For concreteness, in an AFFINE basis consisting of two target-plane
directions and d, write P(x,y,z)=(x,y,0). This basis is not required to be
orthonormal. Projected quadruples (0,0),(2,0),(2,2),(0,2) give a quadrilateral;
(0,0),(2,0),(0,2),(1/2,1/2) give an interior-point triangle;
the fourth point (1,0) instead gives an edge-point triangle; the fourth
point (0,0) instead gives a coincident-pair triangle. Lift the fourth point
by a nonzero d-height and leave the first three at height zero.

For the tetrahedron EDGE FRAME, the exact image is
`[A_1B_1] union [A_1C_1] union [A_1D_1] union [B_1C_1] union [B_1D_1] union [C_1D_1]`.
Some segments may collapse or overlap. It is not merely the perimeter of
the triangle or quadrilateral and is not generally the filled region.

The original B07 quadrilateral assertion is a particular example, not an
exhaustive theorem for every tetrahedron. B08 noninjectivity also has an
independent proof: an interior point x admits distinct x and x+epsilon*d
within K for sufficiently small nonzero epsilon, and P identifies them.
For the surface, take endpoints of an interior fibre instead.

### 153(4): Parallelepiped

For independent u,v,w write

`K = q + [0,1]u + [0,1]v + [0,1]w`.

This follows from the parallelogram faces and equal parallel transverse
edges in the original definition; it does not make the solid a cube.
With a=T(u), b=T(v), c=T(w),

`P(K) = P(q) + [0,1]a + [0,1]b + [0,1]c`.

The three projected vectors span the target plane because u,v,w span the
original space and T has rank 2. At most one of a,b,c can vanish; two
independent original vectors cannot both lie in the one-dimensional kernel.
Group the nonzero generators by UNORIENTED parallel direction:

- Two directions: the image is a nondegenerate parallelogram. This occurs
  if one generator vanishes, or if two nonzero generators are parallel
  (possibly oppositely directed). Their interval sum is one interval in
  that direction; the third supplies the other direction.
- Three directions: the image is a convex centrally symmetric hexagon.
  Each direction yields exactly two opposite boundary edges.

Proof of the side count: for a supporting linear functional lambda, each
interval contributes its positive endpoint or negative endpoint according
to the sign of lambda(generator). At a zero sign for one direction, that
interval is free and contributes a boundary edge. With three distinct
directions, lambda crosses six distinct signed perpendicular directions,
giving six edges; with two directions it gives four. The centre is
`P(q)+(a+b+c)/2`, because replacing each coefficient t by 1-t preserves the
sum set and reflects about this point. Thus no triangle, pentagon, octagon,
segment, or point is possible for a genuine three-dimensional parallelepiped.
Additional collinear projected vertices do not create additional polygon
sides.

Both classes exist. In the same affine basis as above, choose u=d and two
independent target-plane generators for a parallelogram. To obtain a
hexagon, take u=(1,0,0), v=(0,1,0), w=(1,1,1). The lifts are independent,
and their three projected directions are distinct. A four-sided case with
no zero generator is u=(1,0,0), v=(1,0,1), w=(0,1,0).

For the SURFACE the boundary-fibre proof gives the same filled region.
For the twelve-edge FRAME, project each edge: for i=1,2,3 and each choice
of the other two coefficients in {0,1}, the segment runs from
`P(q)+sum(other coefficients * generators)` to that point plus generator i.
This is a finite union of 12 possibly overlapping/collapsed segments, not
the entire filled parallelogram or hexagon.

## Author Derivation: Centre Symmetry and Invariance

For the prose centre O, define author notation
`Z_O(x)=2o-x`, where o is the position vector of O.

- If x != o, the unique image lies on line Ox on the opposite ray at the
  same distance from O. Solving the midpoint equation gives `x'=2o-x`;
  uniqueness follows from this equation.
- If x=o, there is no need to select a line or opposite ray: the source
  explicitly stipulates Z_O(O)=O.
- Injectivity: `2o-x=2o-y` implies x=y.
- Surjectivity: for any y, use x=2o-y.
- Involution: `Z_O(Z_O(x))=x`. Its inverse exists on the WHOLE space and
  equals itself, unlike an arbitrary merely injective self-map.
- `Z_O(x)-Z_O(y)=-(x-y)`, preserving distances, collinearity, incidence,
  parallel directions, and affine combinations. In particular the image of
  a tetrahedron is the tetrahedron of the four image vertices.

For a point x, setwise invariance of {x} is pointwise invariance and means
`2o-x=x`, hence x=o. O is the only fixed point.

For a line `ell=a+span(v)`, v != 0,
`Z_O(ell)=2o-a+span(v)`. Equality with ell holds exactly when
`o-a belongs to span(v)`, i.e. O is ON the line. Otherwise the image line
is distinct, with the same direction; the two are parallel even under a
strict convention. If O is on the line they coincide, which is also
"parallel" under this book's inclusive convention.

For a plane `beta={x:n.x=c}`,
`Z_O(beta)={x:n.x=2n.o-c}`.
It equals beta exactly when `n.o=c`, i.e. O belongs to beta. Otherwise
the two planes are distinct and parallel. These necessity arguments do
not rely on a diagram or only prove existence.

Lines and planes through O are SETWISE fixed, not pointwise fixed.
Each noncentre point on them is exchanged with another point of the same
set. For any subset Q, `Z_O(Q)=Q` precisely when Q is closed under the
pairing x <-> 2o-x; arbitrary unions of such pairs, with O optionally
included, give all invariant subsets. Empty Q is also invariant. Invariant
affine subspaces are exactly those containing O; a point, line, plane,
and the whole space are the corresponding dimension cases.

### 158(2): f inverse circle f and the Literal Definition Defect

The ORIGINAL B16 definition says f:S->S is injective. It does NOT state
that f(S)=S. The ORIGINAL B20 says the swapped ordered pairs determine
another space transformation. Under B16 alone that global assertion is
not justified.

Explicit counterexample, requiring no continuity assumptions:

`g(t)=t if t<0; g(t)=t+1 if t>=0`,
`f(x,y,z)=(g(x),y,z)`.

g is injective: it is injective on each half-line and their images
(-infinity,0) and [1,infinity) are disjoint. Thus f is an injective
S-to-S map satisfying the literal definition. Its image omits the slab
`0<=x<1`. There is no preimage, and hence no swapped-graph inverse value,
at (1/2,0,0). The inverse is not a total map S->S.

What always exists is the unique inverse
`h:f(S)->S`, with `h(f(x))=x`. Thus `h circle f=E_S` on ALL S even
without surjectivity. Its opposite composition is
`f circle h=E_{f(S)}`, identity only on the image.

Accordingly the requested expression has two explicitly separated readings:

- Reading f^{-1} as this image-domain inverse: `f^{-1} circle f=E_S`.
  This cancellation is valid for the literal injective definition.
- Insisting f^{-1} is itself a GLOBAL space transformation as B20 claims:
  existence needs the additional condition f(S)=S. With that condition
  the answer is E_S; without it the asserted global f^{-1} may not exist.

This is an author disclosure of a literal mathematical gap, not an erratum
entry, source correction, inserted "onto" condition, or modern edit.

## Composition, Graphs, and Identity: Full Body Closure

For a mapping Phi->F, each original point has exactly one image, but the
image set `f(Phi)` need not fill F. B07's subset symbol is inclusive in
its stated usage because equality is explicitly allowed. Surjectivity
means `f(Phi)=F`; injectivity compares two domain points, not merely
membership of the image in F.

The graph `{(x,f(x)):x in S}` gives the forward map. Swapping pairs is
single-valued on the image exactly because f is injective; it is total
on S exactly when f is also onto. No two distinct preimages are silently
selected. For bijections, inverse image of a shape agrees with the
shape's set-theoretic preimage; absent surjectivity its inverse-domain
restriction must remain explicit.

For injective self-maps f1,f2, the composite `f2 circle f1` is total and
injective: equal final images imply equal f1 images by injectivity of f2,
then equal original points by injectivity of f1. This does not establish
surjectivity. For a shape Q its image is `f2(f1(Q))`. If both maps are
bijective, their composite is bijective and its inverse is
`f1^{-1} circle f2^{-1}`; successive cancellation proves this on all S.
For merely injective maps that inverse formula is valid only on
`f2(f1(S))`, with the intermediate inverse domains restricted accordingly.
