## Task 1

Determine the physical mechanism by which a current can heat a conductor.
In a metal, an electric field acts on free electrons. Their directed motion and
interactions with the material's particles transfer energy supplied electrically
to the material's internal energy. Increased thermal motion can increase its
temperature. Charges are carriers, not fuel that vanishes as heat.
The lesson's generated heat is the energy converted internally by the current;
some can subsequently leave the conductor for its surroundings. Generated heat
therefore need not equal the conductor's net increase of internal energy.
Distinguish directed charge transport from random thermal motion, and electrical
energy conversion from accumulation of internal energy.
Independent explanatory writing is part of the source task.

## Task 2

Determine generated heat in a stationary purely resistive conductor when voltage
and current remain constant throughout the timed interval.
Electrical work is A = U I t. There is no mechanical or chemical energy output
in the stipulated pure-resistor model, so Q = A = U I t.
Use U in volts, I in amperes and t in seconds; Q is in joules because
volt * ampere * second = watt * second = joule.
This computes generated heat, not necessarily the conductor's retained internal
energy. Do not apply the equality of all electrical work with heat indiscriminately
to a motor or an electrochemical device.

## Task 3

Determine the expression using current, resistance and duration, and produce the
substitution and simplification independently.
For the ohmic stationary pure resistor, U = I R.
Substitute into Q = U I t:

Q = (I R) I t = I * I * R * t = I^2 R t.

The second factor of I already belongs to U I t; it must not be discarded.
For one whole interval, I and R must be constant or approximately so.
With amperes, ohms and seconds, A^2 * ohm * s = watt * s = joule.
Recognition of this derivation does not demonstrate independently writing it.

## Task 4

State Joule-Lenz law and the controlled conditions of its proportionalities:
Q is proportional to I^2, R and t in the stated pure-resistor regime.
When comparing I, hold R and t fixed: replacing I by k I gives k^2 Q.
When comparing R, hold I and t fixed: replacing R by k R gives k Q.
When comparing t, hold I and R fixed: replacing t by k t gives k Q.
In each full timed run, use constant or approximately constant I and R.
Actual R can depend on temperature; a statement based only on an initial R
cannot be transferred without qualification to a changing-temperature run.
Holding voltage fixed is not holding current fixed: at fixed U and t,
I = U/R and Q = U^2 t/R, so increasing R decreases generated heat in this model.
The qualitative law, exponent, conditions and independent written explanation
are distinct requirements; none is just a separate blank.

## Task g2-1

Determine heat generated by a 20-ohm resistance carrying 5 amperes for
30 minutes, with current and resistance unchanged.
Convert the complete interval first: t = 30 * 60 = 1800 s.
Power = I^2 R = 5^2 * 20 = 500 W.
Q = 500 * 1800 = 900000 J = 9.0 * 10^5 J.
The quantity is generated energy, not a temperature rise. No calorie conversion
or material heat capacity is required. Independent calculation and unit handling
are production abilities of the written task.

## Task g2-2

Explain why a wire joint needs a sound, durable connection rather than only a
simple twist. The source gives soldering as an example, not a learner action.
A loose or inadequate contact can have appreciable and unstable contact
resistance. For comparison at the same current, its local generated power is
I^2 R_contact. Reducing and stabilizing contact resistance by a sound joint
reduces local overheating for that comparison and helps prevent intermittent
contact; poor contact can also cause arcing or loss of continuity.
This is a local same-current comparison, not a claim that every whole circuit
keeps exactly the same current when its total resistance changes.
Sound connection does not mean unlimited current is safe or that insulation
and mechanical security no longer matter.
Preserve the original principle-only restriction: no self-directed soldering,
disassembly or assembly of a supply circuit. A choice cannot demonstrate
constructing a safe joint or independently explaining the physics.

## Task g2-3

Explain the possible rapid failure of an immersion heater taken out of water
without first disconnecting the supply.
While still energized, the resistive element continues receiving electrical
energy. Water normally takes heat away effectively. In air that cooling is
greatly reduced, so generated energy can exceed energy carried away, internal
energy accumulates and the element's temperature can rise to damaging values.
The source says it may fail, not that every apparatus must fail identically.
Resistance and power may change with temperature; neither constant power nor
a suddenly increased current is necessary for the explanation. Heat generation
and heat removal are different quantities.
This is fault-scenario reasoning only. Do not perform an energized out-of-water
experiment; disconnect first, and do not claim clicks demonstrate practical
fault reproduction or safe operation.

## Task g2-4

Determine which of three equal-length, equal-cross-section copper, steel and
copper-nickel-alloy wires in series generates most heat during the same interval,
explain the conclusion, and preserve the conditional teacher demonstration.

Let common length be L, cross-section be S, and wire j's actual resistivity at
its own working temperature be rho_j(T_j). Its resistance is
R_j = rho_j(T_j) L/S. Series connection supplies the same instantaneous current
to all three wires, not generally the same voltage across each.
For nonzero constant current and constant or approximately constant resistances,
Q_j = I^2 R_j t = I^2 t (L/S) rho_j(T_j).
Thus Q_j/Q_k = rho_j(T_j)/rho_k(T_k): the wire with strictly greatest actual
working-temperature resistivity generates strictly most heat. Equal current
alone does not imply equal heat; voltage drops are I R_j and generally differ.

No exact steel or copper-nickel alloy grade, actual working temperatures,
resistivity data, or measured resistances are supplied. Material names alone do
not specify those quantities. A universal named-material ordering is not derived
from the supplied data. Do not invent numeric resistivities or assert an
arbitrary-alloy ranking is universally true.
The exact named-material winner is unresolved and substantively deferred.
The invariant comparison remains assessable by an explicitly conditional
constant/approximately-constant-resistance model, not by pretending that this
model has measured the missing resistivities.

If heating changes the resistances substantially, the general comparison is
Q_j = integral I(t)^2 R_j(T_j(t)) dt over the common interval. A single initial
resistivity ranking does not settle that integral when rankings can change.
The constant-resistance assessment is an added visible qualification, not a
