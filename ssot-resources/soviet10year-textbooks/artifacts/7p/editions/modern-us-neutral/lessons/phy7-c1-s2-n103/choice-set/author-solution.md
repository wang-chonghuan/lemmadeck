## Source 1

Task: relate temperature to thermal molecular motion for the SAME liquid.

For the same kind of liquid, higher temperature ordinarily means greater average
thermal kinetic energy and faster thermal motion on average. The molecular speeds
are distributed: an average does not prescribe one speed for every molecule.
There is no numerical value or individual-molecule speed to calculate. The
same-liquid condition prevents treating temperature as a direct ranking of speeds
for molecules of different masses or kinds.

Distinction: ensemble-average thermal motion versus identical individual speeds.
Relevant definition: block 0; block 3 connects it to surface escape.

## Source 2

Task: define evaporation, including initial state and location.

Evaporation is the transfer of molecules from the surface of a liquid to its gas
state. It is a liquid-surface process and need not wait for boiling. A generic
object losing material is not enough: solid-to-gas transfer without a liquid
stage is sublimation, not evaporation.

Distinction: liquid-surface-to-gas transfer, not just any transfer into air.
Relevant definitions: blocks 0 and 10.

## Source 3

Task: explain why a liquid can evaporate below its boiling point, using motion.

Even below boiling, molecules do not all have the same speed or kinetic energy.
Some surface molecules have enough energy to overcome nearby molecular attraction
and leave the liquid. A whole-liquid boiling threshold is unnecessary for those
individual surface departures. The material must still be liquid; this does not
rename sublimation as evaporation.

Distinction: some energetic surface molecules versus all molecules waiting for
a common threshold. Relevant definitions: blocks 0 and 3.

## Source 4

Task: name vapor-to-liquid transfer.

It is condensation. Molecules from the vapor enter the liquid phase. This direction
is the reverse of evaporation. Both transfers can occur at the same time; naming
one does not establish that the other has stopped.

Distinction: direction of phase transfer, not whether molecules cross a surface.
Relevant definition: block 4.

## Source 5

Task: enumerate the factors taught here and explain why each affects the rate.

The complete taught list has four factors:

- Liquid kind: at otherwise comparable conditions, easier escape against
  intermolecular attraction ordinarily gives faster evaporation.
- Temperature of the same liquid: higher temperature ordinarily increases average
  thermal kinetic energy and the supply of surface molecules able to escape.
- Airflow near the surface: at comparable liquid temperature, moving air carries
  vapor away, reduces molecules returning to the liquid and ordinarily increases
  net liquid loss.
- Exposed liquid surface area: at otherwise comparable conditions, a larger surface
  offers more molecules the opportunity to leave per unit time. It does not require
  increasing each molecule's kinetic energy at a fixed temperature.

Use net loss = outward transfer minus returning transfer for the drying statement.
Ambient vapor concentration can therefore affect net drying; block 7 expressly
allows no net loss or net condensation in sufficiently vapor-rich air. This is
not an additional omitted source task: the four-item enumeration is the lesson's
explicit list, with this air-condition qualification preserved in the question.
Do not claim that wind works by warming the liquid, that the area change warms it,
or that different liquids share identical evaporation behavior at one temperature.

Distinction: per-molecule escape propensity, number of available surface molecules
and return flow are different causal mechanisms. Relevant definitions:
blocks 0, 2, 3, 7 and 9.
