1. In a stationary liquid under gravity, pressure acts in every direction
   and increases with depth. For the source's horizontal-faced rectangular
   block, equal opposite side forces cancel. The bottom is deeper than the
   top, so its upward pressure force exceeds the top's downward force:
   F=F2-F1, directed upward. With equal horizontal area S and uniform liquid
   density, F=(rho*g*h2-rho*g*h1)*S=rho*g*(h2-h1)*S. Pascal's directional
   transmission alone is not the depth law; both facts are needed. This is a
   full explanation, not just the equation or the word "upward."
2. Hang the same object from a spring, mark its extension in air, then immerse
   it in water without bottom/wall support. Original163a has the longer
   spring/lower pointer;163b shows the shorter spring/higher pointer with the
   object submerged. Its weight has not changed; the liquid adds an upward
   force, reducing the support force. In static equilibrium T+Fb=mg, so the
   tension decrease measures the change in buoyancy from air to water.
   Hand support producing the same shortening is the source's comparison.
   No numeric force or extension is supplied or invented.
3. Valid examples include a released submerged cork rising, a stone feeling
   easier to lift underwater, and a boat floating on water. A lighter-than-air
   balloon is also valid when its net buoyancy exceeds the relevant total
   weight; buoyancy exists even for a body that sinks. Each example needs its
   link to upward fluid force, not an unsupported claim that every immersed
   body rises. Open personal examples are acceptable, not a unique closed key.
4. The source comparison balances a sealed large flask/glass sphere against
   weights in air, then surrounds it with denser carbon dioxide in the large
   vessel. Original164 shows the flask side elevated and the weights side
   lower. The sealed object's mass is unchanged while surrounding-gas
   buoyancy increases; its apparent supported weight falls and the balance
   tips accordingly. This is a historical/theoretical experimental account,
   not directions for a child to handle gas or laboratory apparatus.
   Weights also have gas buoyancy; the stated observation assumes the flask's
   much larger displaced volume and the source arrangement. No density,
   balance angle or measured force is invented.
