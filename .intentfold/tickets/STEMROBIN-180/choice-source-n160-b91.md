## Independent Solutions

### Q1: Demonstrate a Force Experimentally

Suspend conductor AB from flexible supply leads between the horseshoe magnet's
poles so it can move, using the nonparallel arrangement of fig-345. With the
circuit open, there is no winding/conductor current and no current-dependent
magnetic force. Close the circuit without changing the support or magnet:
movement/deflection of AB is evidence of a force on the energized conductor.
Reversing the current with the magnetic arrangement unchanged reverses motion
and strengthens the causal comparison. The source conclusion concerns this
arrangement, not every orientation: a conductor parallel to the magnetic field
has zero such force. The picture identifies open state a and closed state b;
it does not supply a calibrated displacement or an absolute force vector.

### Q2: What Determines Force Direction?

For the same conductor geometry and a nonzero magnetic force, direction depends
on both the current direction and the magnetic-field direction. Reverse current
alone while retaining the field: force/motion reverses (block 7). Reverse the
field by interchanging magnet poles while retaining current: force reverses
(block 8). These are controlled, separate changes; changing a current magnitude
is not the stated reversal operation. A zero magnetic force in the parallel
case has no direction to reverse. No absolute in/out-of-page force direction
or named hand-rule recall is required by the selected task.

### Q3: Motion of the Original Frame

Use the source context of fig-346: a light rectangular winding on a vertical
shaft, between the transverse pole field, initially with its plane parallel
to the field lines. On closing the circuit, forces on the winding produce a
turning couple. The frame turns toward the equilibrium orientation in which
its plane is perpendicular to the field lines (the dashed frame, block 13).
This is a quarter-turn to the first equilibrium orientation, not a half-turn
to the first switching event. "Frame plane parallel" does not mean every wire
segment is parallel to the field. Zero turning moment at equilibrium likewise
does not mean every wire segment has zero force.

With unchanged winding-current direction, inertia can carry the frame beyond
equilibrium, after which the magnetic turning action is restoring rather than
continually driving the same rotation. The source does not specify an exact
overshoot, damping, stopping time, or absolute clockwise/counterclockwise
answer. It neither proves an instantaneous stop at equilibrium nor sustained
one-way rotation with the two complete supply rings shown in fig-346.

### Q4: Sustain That Same Frame's Rotation

"The previous problem" means the entire Q3 arrangement, including the vertical
shaft, transverse magnetic field, and initially field-parallel frame plane.
Keep the winding powered in that field. Let inertia carry the frame through
each equilibrium orientation and reverse winding current at the appropriate
passage, repeating at half-turn intervals between successive passages. The
reversal changes the winding-side forces so the turning action continues in
the original rotational sense after the frame has passed equilibrium.

The first passage from the stated initial orientation is a quarter-turn;
"every 180 degrees" is the interval between later reversals, not an instruction
to wait for the first 180 degrees from that initial position. One reversal,
arbitrary-time reversal, inertia alone, or two complete supply rings without
commutation does not implement the stated continuous-rotation method.

### Q5: Automatic Current Reversal

A commutator provides rotation-synchronized automatic reversal of the winding
current. The simplest source example is the pair of insulated half-rings
rotating with the frame, contacted by fixed supply brushes (block 16,
fig-347/348). The operation reverses current in the winding, not the external
battery polarity or the magnet poles. The complete rings and brushes of
fig-346 provide electrical connection but do not themselves exchange the
winding ends' source polarity on a half-turn.

### Q6: Construction and Operation

The simplest commutator has two conducting half-rings, mutually insulated,
on the frame's shaft and rigidly rotating with it. Connect one winding end to
each half-ring. Fixed brushes connect the two half-rings to opposite supply
terminals. The insulated separation prevents the halves from acting as one
shorted conductor.

In fig-347, fixed brush 1 is on the left negative lead and brush 2 on the
right positive lead. E is the left half-ring connected to winding end A;
F is the right half-ring connected to winding end D. Tracing conventional
current gives:

`+ -> brush 2 -> F -> D -> C -> B -> A -> E -> brush 1 -> -`.

In fig-348 the fixed supply/brush positions are unchanged, but F is on the
left and E on the right, with E still connected to A and F to D. The trace is:

`+ -> brush 2 -> E -> A -> B -> C -> D -> F -> brush 1 -> -`.

Thus the direction through the identified winding reverses:
`D-C-B-A` becomes `A-B-C-D`. The spatial upper-wire arrow still points left,
the lower-wire arrow still points right, and the curved rotation arrow retains
its pictured sense. Material-winding current reversal must not be confused
with reversal of the external supply or reversal of mechanical rotation.
The contact exchange repeats every half-turn at the appropriate phase
described in block 14. Inertia carries the frame across the zero-turning-moment
position and, in an idealized split-ring account, any brief contact transition.
The source supplies no gap angle, brush width, transition duration, starting
torque at a dead position, or numerical torque.
