## Whole Original Tasks

### Task 1: Receiver And Transmitter

The receiver is the ear-side listening device: it converts incoming current
changes into diaphragm vibration, then vibration of nearby air, producing sound.
The transmitter, also called a microphone, is the mouth-side speaking device:
sound-induced vibration becomes variation of an electrical signal. In this
lesson's carbon transmitter, that conversion is modulation of supplied current
through changing carbon resistance. The two devices have complementary
conversion directions; their positions alone are not their functional
definitions.

### Task 2: Transmitter Construction And Operation

Figure 342 and block 1 specify casing 1; carbon shoe 2 with a recess holding
carbon powder 5; fixed electrode 3 at the casing bottom; insulating pad 4 between
shoe and casing; and diaphragm 6, made of steel or pressed carbon sheet. The
fixed electrode is also insulated from the casing. Naming carbon and a
diaphragm without their mounting and insulating relationships is incomplete.

In the powered simplified circuit, current passes through diaphragm, carbon
shoe, carbon powder and electrode 3. With supply and other conditions unchanged,
absence of acoustic vibration leaves carbon resistance and current constant:
the qualitative AB region of figure 343. Sound vibrates the diaphragm, changing
granule contact and pressure, hence resistance and current strength. The current
variation reflects the original vibration and reaches the remote receiver. BC
illustrates strength variation on a DC basis, not repeated current reversal.
The power supply provides the electrical energy; sound controls the variation.
No graph scale permits a numerical current, period, frequency or time reading.

### Task 3: Receiver Construction And Operation

Figure 344 and block 15 specify circular thin steel diaphragm 1; casing 2;
permanent magnet 3 with two pole shoes; fine-wire coils 4 around the poles; and
cover 5 clamping the diaphragm to the casing. A small gap remains between the
diaphragm and pole shoes. The carbon shoe/powder apparatus belongs to the
transmitter, not this receiver.

Even without coil current, the permanent magnet attracts the steel diaphragm,
making it slightly concave. With constant current, coil and permanent-magnet
fields combine and the diaphragm maintains a static position in the stated
model. Varying current changes the added coil field and the resulting
attractive force. The diaphragm then vibrates in response to the electrical
variation and sets nearby air vibrating, recreating the sound. A constant force
or static deflection alone is not sustained speech reproduction; current
variation, rather than current reversal or disappearance of the permanent
magnet's field, supplies the changing action.

### Task 4: Complete Conversation Chain

For one speaking direction: vocal-cord vibration -> local air vibration ->
transmitter diaphragm vibration -> changing carbon-granule contacts and
pressure -> changing resistance -> variation of power-supplied current ->
telephone line -> remote receiver coils -> changing added magnetic field acting
with the permanent magnet -> changing attraction on the steel diaphragm ->
receiver diaphragm vibration -> new local air vibration -> listener's ear and
hearing system. Thus the line conveys electrical variation, not the original
airborne sound. When the other person speaks, their transmitter and the first
person's receiver perform the same complementary roles. The supplied simplified
model suffices; an unprovided exchange, switching scheme or modern telephone
architecture is not needed.
