# Musical Tones and Noise: Loudness and Pitch

We hear many different sounds every day.
Singing, taut instrument strings, and whistles can give musical tones.
Explosions and a running internal-combustion engine can give noise.
Hissing snakes and barking or howling dogs are further examples.
Our vocal organs can make musical tones as well as noise.
This familiar contrast leads us to examine the vibrations.

**Musical tones** How do musical tones differ physically from noise?
Why do different musical tones sound different?

An almost pure tone can be made with a **tuning fork**.
Figure 81 shows a fork fixed to a wooden box open at the top.
A light hammer strikes one of its two arms.
The vibrating arms produce sound waves.
Their vibrations gradually die away.
The sound becomes quieter as the vibration amplitude falls.
To record the motion, attach a needle to one arm.
Let it slide uniformly along a soot-blackened glass plate.
Its vibration leaves a trace nearly shaped like a sine curve.
Figure 82 shows this trace.
Uniform sliding spreads the motion out in time along the plate.
This is called a **time trace** of the vibration.
It resembles the pattern made with a sand pendulum.
The nearly sinusoidal trace shows nearly harmonic vibration.
This is how the tuning fork's motion is identified.

**Pure tone** Sound from harmonic vibration is called a pure tone.
This ideal sound has a single frequency.
A general musical tone may contain several related frequencies.

We hear differences in both loudness and pitch.
Loudness tells us how loud a sound seems, pitch how high it seems.

**Loudness** is how loud a sound seems to a listener.
Compare the same fork at the same listening position.
A harder strike makes the vibration amplitude larger.
The fork then generally sounds louder.
This comparison keeps the frequency and listening conditions the same.
Loudness also depends on how the ear responds.

Our ears are not equally sensitive to all frequencies.
Equal sound-wave amplitudes need not sound equally loud.
Changing frequency can change the loudness we hear.
The source places greatest sensitivity near 3500 Hz.
This is an approximate description, not a fixed rule.
Sensitivity depends on the listener and sound level.

**Pitch** is how high or low a tone sounds.
Compare tuning forks of different sizes.
Attach a needle to an arm of each sounding fork.
Record each motion on soot-blackened glass.
Use the same uniform sliding speed for each record.
The higher tone gives a sine-like trace with a shorter period.
A shorter period means more vibrations in the same time.
Its vibration frequency is therefore higher.
For a pure tone, frequency determines pitch.

A vibrating string shows the same link to pitch.
Keep the string and its vibrating length unchanged.
Increasing its tension raises its natural frequency.
A guitar's tuning peg can tighten the string.
The tighter string vibrates faster.
It therefore produces a higher tone.

**Noise** Typical noise mixes many frequencies irregularly.
It has no single stable pitch.
Musical tones can also mix frequencies, in a repeating pattern.
Such a pattern can give a definite pitch.
Some real sounds combine a tone with noise.
The distinction is not simply loud versus quiet.
