# 84. Light Interference

<!-- source_refs: p0225#6; kind: p -->
We will now study phenomena that provide evidence that light behaves as a wave.
As discussed earlier, waves can show interference and diffraction: waves that meet can reinforce or weaken one another, and waves passing through a narrow opening or around an obstacle can spread out.
Observing interference and diffraction in light therefore provides evidence that **light shows wave properties as it travels**.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#7; kind: p -->
People saw light interference long before they knew how to explain it.
A soap bubble can show colored bands, and a thin film of kerosene or petroleum on water can show rainbow colors;

<!-- source_refs: p0225#7; kind: p -->
these are familiar interference patterns.
In the passage quoted by the textbook, Mark Twain describes soap bubbles: "Soap bubbles floating through the air ... sparkle with the magnificent colors of the objects around them. A soap bubble is perhaps nature's most wonderful and most delicate miracle."
Light interference is an important reason why soap bubbles look so colorful.

---

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
The textbook credits the British scientist Thomas Young with an important early explanation of the colors of thin oil films, describing him as the first to reach this ingenious conclusion.
His explanation was that wave $1$ and wave $2$ combine where they meet, producing different colors in the reflected light.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
Wave $1$ reflects from the outer surface of the oil film, while wave $2$ first refracts into the film, reflects from its inner surface, and then emerges;
Figure 198 shows these two paths.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
**Light interference is the superposition of two light waves, making the resulting light oscillation stronger or weaker at different points in space.**
At the same point, oscillations that act together produce a stronger combined oscillation, while oscillations that act against each other produce a weaker one, making that point brighter or darker.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
Weaker light does not necessarily mean no light at all;
complete cancellation requires equal wave amplitudes as well as oscillations that exactly oppose each other.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
The film's thickness and the light's wavelength affect the result because they change how far each wave has progressed through its oscillation when the waves meet.
A wave's phase tells us which stage of a complete oscillation cycle it has reached;

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
comparing the phases where the waves meet tells us whether they reinforce or weaken each other.
Traveling an extra distance delays the oscillation, but the difference in geometric distance is not the whole phase difference, because light travels at different speeds in different media and reflection can also change its phase.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
For light incident nearly straight onto an interface between transparent, nonabsorbing media, reflection from a lower-refractive-index medium toward a higher-index medium reverses the oscillation by half a cycle;
reflection in the opposite direction does not produce this reversal.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
When using the textbook's rules below, the delay must therefore include both the phase delay from propagation and the phase changes from reflection along the two paths, not just the extra distance traveled inside the film.
Expressing this total phase difference as an equivalent distance, using the wavelength in the same medium at the comparison point, gives the effective delay used here;

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
an effective delay of one wavelength represents a phase difference of one complete cycle.
The following rules describe two steady, periodic light waves compared at the same observation point, with the same frequency and aligned oscillation directions, whose phase relationship remains stable throughout the observation.

<!-- source_refs: p0225#8, p0225#11, p0226#1; kind: p -->
Within this model and total-phase convention, if wave $2$, which enters and returns from the film, has an effective delay relative to reflected wave $1$ equal to an integer number of wavelengths, their oscillations act together and the light is **reinforced**.
If the second wave's effective delay is half a wavelength, or an odd number of half-wavelengths, their oscillations oppose each other and the light is **weakened**.
This is not a geometric-distance rule that can be applied directly to every oil film: first determine whether reflection reverses the phase at each interface, using the media on either side of the film, and then compare the waves' total phases.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#2; kind: p -->
For a stable interference pattern to form, the two waves must be **coherent**.
For the same monochromatic light compared in the same medium, this means that the waves have the same wavelength and a constant phase difference at the same observation point.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#2; kind: p -->
The same wavelength here also means the same frequency, while a constant phase difference means that one wave's lead or delay within the oscillation cycle does not keep changing relative to the other wave.
The waves reflected from the outer and inner surfaces of the oil film are **two parts of the same light beam**, so they can retain a shared phase relationship.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#2; kind: p -->
This also requires that the difference between the paths is not so large that the relationship is lost, and that the film and paths remain sufficiently stable during observation.
Under these conditions, reinforcement or weakening at the same point persists, so the light-and-dark pattern does not continually shift.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#2; kind: p -->
Two ordinary independent light sources do not share this phase relationship, and the phase difference between their waves changes continually.
Over an ordinary observation time, the changing reinforcement and weakening average out, so no stable interference pattern is visible.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#3; kind: p -->
The textbook also explains that Young recognized that **differences in light's color are related to differences in wavelength or frequency**.
When compared in the same medium, monochromatic visible light of different wavelengths corresponds to different colors, while frequency describes how quickly the light's oscillation cycles repeat.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#3; kind: p -->
At an interface, the frequency stays the same, but the propagation speed and wavelength can change, so the medium must be specified when relating wavelength to color.
White light contains many colors, and therefore contains light waves with many different frequencies.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#3; kind: p -->
For fixed media and a fixed direction of illumination, the film-thickness conditions for reinforcing or weakening reflected waves depend on their wavelength;
these conditions must still include the phase changes caused by reflection.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#3; kind: p -->
A single part of a film can therefore reinforce some reflected colors while weakening others, rather than making every color brighter or darker in the same way.
If the thickness differs from one part of the film to another, the combination of reinforced and weakened colors differs too.
This is why a film of uneven thickness displays different colors under white light.

---

<!-- source_refs: p0226#4; kind: p -->
Knowing that color is related to light's frequency and wavelength lets us understand dispersion more deeply than in the previous unit.
We must consider not only which color the light appears to be, but also how its refractive index in a medium depends on these physical properties.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#5; kind: p -->
**Dispersion is the dependence of light's refractive index on its oscillation frequency or wavelength.**
The refractive index describes a medium's effect on the speed of light, and light of different frequencies can have different refractive indices in the same medium.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#5; kind: p -->
When describing this dependence using wavelength, specify whether the comparison uses wavelengths in vacuum or in a particular medium;
a wavelength change on entering another medium does not mean that the light's color has changed.
A refractive index that varies with frequency can cause different colors to bend by different amounts during refraction, linking the separation of colors in the previous unit to the physical properties of light waves.

In its account of scientific history, the textbook describes Thomas Young (1773-1829) as an outstanding British scientist with unusually broad interests and many talents.
He was a well-known physician, an insightful physicist and astronomer, and also worked in mechanical engineering and metallurgy.

<!-- source_refs: p0226#6, p0226#8; kind: p -->
He was an Egyptologist and a physiologist as well: these fields study ancient Egypt's history and culture, and how living bodies work, respectively.
He knew many languages, and the textbook also praises him as a gifted musician and a talented gymnast.

<!-- source_refs: p0227#1; kind: p -->
The textbook lists the discovery of light interference and the explanation of diffraction in terms of waves among Young's main achievements, and credits him with the term **interference**.
This concerns discovering and scientifically explaining the phenomena, not a claim that nobody had seen the colors of soap bubbles or oil films before him.
The textbook also records that Young was the first to measure the wavelength of light, not only explaining light in terms of waves but also measuring the distance associated with one light-wave cycle.
