# Newton's Rings

Place a plano-convex lens on a flat glass plate with its curved face downward, using a lens whose curved surface has a large radius of curvature.
A very thin air layer remains between the lens and the plate, and its thickness changes with distance from their contact point.
Under suitable illumination, two reflected beams interfere and form concentric rings called **Newton's rings**.

Use a lens with a gently curved surface and carefully examine the place where it touches the glass plate, preferably with a magnifying glass.
Under the reflected-light conditions discussed in the source, an ideal contact gives a dark spot near the centre, with small coloured rings around it under white light.

The source explains that Newton (牛顿) did not give the wave explanation used here, whereas Young (杨) explained the rings by treating light as a wave.
Following Young's reasoning, consider light of a definite wavelength incident approximately normally on a plano-convex lens;

Figure 199 shows the light path.
Wave $1$ is reflected at the glass-air boundary on the lens's curved surface, while wave $2$ enters the air layer and is reflected at the lower air-glass boundary.

The reflected beams come from one incident beam and can have the same wavelength and a stable relative phase, which is the coherence needed here.
Phase tells us which stage of a vibration cycle a wave has reached, and a stable phase relationship makes a stable bright-and-dark pattern possible.
The additional distance travelled by wave $2$ compared with wave $1$ changes this relationship, but the phase change caused by reflection must also be included rather than comparing geometrical distances alone.

When propagation and reflection are counted together, a total delay of the second wave relative to the first equivalent to an integer number of whole wavelengths① puts the waves at the same stage of vibration when they meet.
Their superposition then strengthens the resultant vibration and gives a brighter region.
This whole-wavelength condition concerns the total delay including reflection, not merely the additional geometrical distance through the air layer.

① The source footnote points out that the effective path difference of wave $1$ and wave $2$ is not simply twice the air-layer thickness at their point of incidence.
Reflection at the lower glass plate changes the wave's phase by $\pi$, which is equivalent, for this comparison, to adding a path difference of $\lambda/2$.

The source does not develop this complication further;
the important point here is why this contribution must enter the total phase delay, not that light physically travels an extra distance.

Conversely, a total delay including reflection equivalent to an odd number of half-wavelengths puts the two waves at opposite stages of vibration when they meet.
Their superposition then reduces the resultant vibration by cancellation and gives a darker region.
Equal vibration strengths allow complete cancellation, while unequal strengths generally allow only partial cancellation.
