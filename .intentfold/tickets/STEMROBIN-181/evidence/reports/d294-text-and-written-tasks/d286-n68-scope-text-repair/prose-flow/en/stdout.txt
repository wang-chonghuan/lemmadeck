[0] Light falling on a surface is usually distributed unevenly.
For example, a lamp suspended above a table often illuminates the center most strongly, while the edges are noticeably dimmer.
Even for a point source, the center receives more luminous flux than the edges, so the difference is not caused solely by the lamp emitting different luminous intensities in different directions.
[1] The ratio of the luminous flux $\Phi$ falling on a surface to its area $S$ gives the average illuminance $E$ over that finite area.
$E=\frac{\Phi}{S}$ (6.5)
When the illumination is uniform, this average also equals the illuminance at every point on the surface.

[5] Place a uniform point source with the same luminous intensity I in every direction at the center of a spherical surface to examine how illuminance depends on distance.
The sphere has surface area $S=4\pi R^2$, and the total luminous flux is $\Phi=4\pi I$ (see equation 6.4).
The illuminance on the sphere is therefore:
$E=\frac{\Phi}{S}=\frac{4\pi I}{4\pi R^2}=\frac{I}{R^2}$
