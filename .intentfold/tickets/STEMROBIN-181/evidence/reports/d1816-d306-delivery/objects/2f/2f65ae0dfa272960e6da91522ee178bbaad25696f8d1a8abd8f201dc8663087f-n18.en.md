# 18. The Oscillating Circuit

> Chapter 2 — Electrical oscillations

The **simplest** system that can produce free electrical oscillations consists of a capacitor and a coil.
The coil connects the capacitor's two plates to form a circuit (Figure 27).
This system is called an **oscillating circuit**.

Figure 27

**Let us see why** oscillations occur in the circuit.
When the capacitor has not been charged and there is no current in the circuit, the system is in equilibrium.
There is no charging or discharging, and no oscillation.

The switch connects the capacitor to a battery pack for a while to charge it (Figure 28a).
The capacitor gains charge and stores energy in its electric field.
This energy is given by the formula:

$E=\frac{q_m^2}{2C}$ (2.1)

Here, E means the energy stored in the capacitor's electric field.
$q_m$ is the capacitor's charge when charging ends, and the maximum charge in this oscillation.
$C$ is its capacitance, which describes its ability to store charge.

At this point, the potential difference between the two plates is $U_m$.
Potential difference is also called voltage, and this symbol means its maximum value.
In these symbols, the subscript m means maximum.

Next, the switch moves to contact 2.
The capacitor starts to discharge, and a current appears in the circuit.

The current does not jump to its maximum value;
it grows gradually.

The reason is self-induction in the coil.
Self-induction is the coil's own induction effect that opposes a change in its current.
As the current appears and grows, the magnetic field it produces also changes.

The changing magnetic field produces a circulating electric field in the wire.
The original book introduced this in ninth grade: it is an electric field produced by a changing magnetic field that circulates along closed paths.
While the magnetic field grows, this electric field points against the current, preventing a sudden increase in current.

As the capacitor discharges, the energy in its electric field decreases.
At the same time, the energy in the magnetic field produced by the current increases.
The magnetic-field energy is given by the formula:

$K=\frac{Li^2}{2}$ (2.2)

Here, K means the energy in the magnetic field.
$i$ is the current at that moment.
$L$ is the coil's self-inductance, also called inductance, which describes how strongly the coil opposes changes in current.

The symbol q means the capacitor's charge at that moment.
When the capacitor has fully discharged, $q=0$, and its electric-field energy is also zero.
If we set aside energy losses for now, conservation of energy means that all the energy is stored in the magnetic field.

The magnetic-field energy is then at its maximum.
The current also reaches its maximum value $I_m$ (Figure 29, a).

This symbol means the maximum current in the oscillation;
the subscript m again means maximum.

Although the potential difference across the coil is zero at this moment, the current cannot stop at once.
Self-induction is again the reason.
It opposes decreases in current as well as increases.

As soon as the current and its magnetic field begin to weaken, a circulating electric field appears along the current's direction.
This time, the electric field helps keep the current flowing.

The continuing current charges the capacitor again.
After this recharge, the signs of the charges on the plates are opposite to their original signs.
The current gradually decreases until it becomes zero.

The magnetic-field energy is then also zero.
If losses are ignored, the capacitor's electric-field energy is again at its maximum.
Energy moved from the electric field to the magnetic field, and has now moved back to the electric field.

After the capacitor charges with reversed polarity, the system has not yet fully returned to its initial state.
Next, the capacitor discharges again and charges with its original polarity.
The charging and discharging process repeats in this way.

If there is no energy loss, the oscillations continue and their amplitude does not decrease.
The recharge with reversed polarity occurs after half a period;

it is not the original complete state.
Only after a full oscillation period do the size and sign of the charge, and the size and direction of the current, return together to their initial state.

A real circuit cannot avoid energy losses completely.
The coil and connecting wires have resistance $R$.
Resistance describes how much they oppose the flow of current.

Because of resistance, some energy in the electromagnetic field becomes internal energy in the wires, making them warmer.
Less energy remains to move back and forth between the electric and magnetic fields.
So real free oscillations gradually decay.
