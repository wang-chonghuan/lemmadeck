# 19. Similarities Between Mechanical and Electrical Oscillations

> Oscillations and Waves | Chapter 1 — Mechanical oscillations

Electrical oscillations in an oscillating circuit are similar to the free oscillations of a spring oscillator, an object attached to a spring.
Different physical quantities change periodically, which means their changes repeat.
In mechanical oscillations, the changing quantities are the object's position coordinate $x$ and its velocity component $v_x$, which gives its speed and direction along the chosen axis.

In electrical oscillations, the changing quantities are the charge $q$ stored in the capacitor and the current $i$ flowing in the circuit.
These quantities have different physical meanings, but the ways they change periodically over time are similar.

This is because the conditions that produce the two kinds of oscillation are similar.
The elastic force $F_x$, exerted by the spring, brings the spring oscillator back toward its equilibrium position, where the spring exerts no net restoring force.

The farther the oscillator moves from equilibrium, the greater the elastic force: the force's magnitude is proportional to the size of the displacement, with spring stiffness $k$ as the constant of proportionality.
The voltage $U$ between the capacitor's plates causes the capacitor to discharge, so current begins to flow.

This voltage is proportional to the charge $q$, because $U=\frac{1}{C}q$.
Here the constant of proportionality is the reciprocal of the capacitance, $\frac{1}{C}$: the capacitance tells us how much charge the capacitor stores for a given voltage.

Because of inertia, the object's tendency to keep its motion, a force can increase the oscillator's speed only gradually;
when the force stops acting, the velocity does not immediately become zero.

Similarly, because of self-induction, in which a changing current produces an effect that opposes that change, the current in a coil increases gradually under an applied voltage;
when the voltage becomes zero, the current does not immediately disappear.

The circuit's inductance $L$, which describes the coil's opposition to changes in current, corresponds to the oscillator's mass $m$.
Comparing the oscillator's kinetic energy $\frac{mv_x^2}{2}$ with the energy of the current's magnetic field, $\frac{Li^2}{2}$, shows how these quantities correspond in the energy formulas.

Charging the capacitor corresponds to moving the oscillator away from equilibrium by hand.
Its initial displacement is $x_{\mathrm{m}}$, so the spring oscillator has potential energy $\frac{kx_{\mathrm{m}}^2}{2}$, energy stored by stretching or compressing the spring (Figure $28$b).

Comparing this expression with the capacitor's energy $\frac{q_{\mathrm{m}}^2}{2C}$ shows that spring stiffness $k$ corresponds to the reciprocal of capacitance, $\frac{1}{C}$.
The oscillator's initial coordinate $x_{\mathrm{m}}$ corresponds to the capacitor's initial charge $q_{\mathrm{m}}$.

The current caused by a potential difference, or voltage, corresponds to the velocity $v_x$ that the oscillator gains under the spring's elastic force (Figure $29$b).
At a particular instant, an alternating current can be expressed as the time derivative of transferred charge: the rate at which charge passes, found by considering shorter and shorter time intervals, with transferred charge counted positively in the chosen positive current direction.
$i=\lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}=q'$.
