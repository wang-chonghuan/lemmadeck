# Measuring the Permeability of Iron

Permeability $\mu$ describes how a material affects a magnetic field;

this lesson retains the source notation for relative permeability compared with the same apparatus without an iron core.

When $\mu$ is much greater than 1, as it is for iron's $\mu$, electromagnetic induction can be used to compare the magnetic flux with and without the core and thereby measure it.

The induced electromotive force is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux through a coil: a faster flux change produces a larger magnitude of induced electromotive force.

When the primary current and apparatus geometry are the same, the increase in magnetic flux density after inserting an iron core can be expressed, in the source notation, as a factor of $\mu$.

This comparison measures the effective relative permeability of this apparatus under those conditions, not a fixed material constant independent of current, core shape and magnetization state.

In Figure 300, the primary coil is connected to a direct-current circuit, and a smaller secondary coil wound around it is connected to a galvanometer.

When the primary circuit is switched on, the current rises from its initial value, changing the magnetic flux through the secondary coil and producing a brief induced current in the secondary circuit.

The galvanometer deflection indicates this induction process;
once the current and flux are steady, switching on no longer produces an induced electromotive force.

Compare switching on without the core and with it, keeping the coil turns and shape, the final primary current and the secondary circuit resistance the same, and fixing the core position when it is present.

For the comparison in this lesson, both measurements start with zero primary current, a demagnetized core and zero initial comparison flux, with background flux removed or subtracted.
The compared flux changes then equal their corresponding final values, without counting uncontrolled remanence as an enhancement.
If the initial flux is nonzero, equal initial values alone do not make the final-flux ratio equal the flux-change ratio.

With the same secondary circuit resistance and after the complete induction pulse has ended, the total charge passing through the galvanometer is proportional to the total change in magnetic flux.

Under these comparison conditions, the flux change and the time integral of induced electromotive force increase by a factor of $\mu$;

this does not mean that the instantaneous induced electromotive force increases by that factor at every moment.

Use a ballistic galvanometer suitable for short charge pulses, starting both measurements with the pointer at rest at zero and using the same calibration, sensitivity and damping conditions.
The entire secondary-current pulse, including its electrical decay, must be much shorter than the pointer's mechanical response time, and both first deflections must lie within the calibrated linear range before their ratio can be used as the total-charge ratio.
Under these conditions, inserting the core increases the deflection by a factor of $\mu$; if the range changes or the calibration is nonlinear, convert both readings to total charge before comparing them.

The source reports that inserting the core increased the measured magnetic flux several thousandfold, showing that iron has a large effective relative permeability under those measurement conditions.

The contribution to the total field from the magnetism of electrons inside the magnetized core is, in this arrangement, in the same direction as the magnetizing coil's field, thereby strengthening it.
