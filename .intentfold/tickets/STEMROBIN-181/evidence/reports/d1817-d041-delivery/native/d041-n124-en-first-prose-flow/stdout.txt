[0] Bohr's theory achieved an important success with hydrogen.
It explained not only which spectral lines hydrogen produces, but also their positions quantitatively.
[1] Applying the same model to helium and more complex atoms did not bring the same success.
The theory still gave important qualitative explanations, but it could not provide an equally reliable, complete quantitative theory.

[2] The difficulty came from an inconsistency within the theory.
It used Newtonian mechanics and Coulomb's law to describe electron motion, while adding quantum postulates that could not be derived from those classical laws.
Physics needed a more unified framework.
During the nineteen-twenties, quantum mechanics took shape and quantum electrodynamics began to develop.

[4] In quantum mechanics, an electron does not follow a definite orbit like a planet.
An **electron cloud** represents the probability of finding the electron in different parts of space, not a trail left by circling motion.①
Imagine dividing space into thin spherical shells centered on the nucleus: for hydrogen in its lowest-energy state, the radial probability distribution for finding the electron in these shells has its maximum near the Bohr radius, corresponding to the radius in (11.8).
This differs from probability density per unit volume, which has its maximum at the nucleus in this state.
Quantum mechanics provides a framework for studying electron clouds, although detailed quantitative calculations can be difficult.
Here we establish the main ideas and leave further detail for later study.
