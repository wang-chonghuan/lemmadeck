## Q1: Full Independent Reflection-Law Solution

The task requires a complete geometric law, not only the equality of two unnamed
angles. For ordinary specular reflection at a smooth surface:

- The incident ray, reflected ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in
  one plane. For non-normal incidence, the incident and reflected ray branches
  lie on opposite sides of that normal in the incidence plane.
- The angle of incidence and angle of reflection are each measured between the
  corresponding ray branch and the local normal, not the tangent surface.
  These two angles are equal.
- At normal incidence both angles are zero and the reflected ray retraces the
  incident straight path in the reverse direction. The incidence plane is then
  not unique, but a plane containing the coincident line and normal exists.
  Do not impose a unique plane or two distinct sides in that degenerate case.

Independent vector check: let n be a unit normal toward the incident medium and
v be the incoming unit propagation direction. Specular reflection preserves the
tangential component and reverses the normal component:

v_reflected = v - 2(v dot n)n.

The reflected direction is in the span of v and n, proving coplanarity. The
incoming ray branch directed away from the surface is -v, whereas the outgoing
branch is v_reflected. Their normal components have the same positive magnitude,
so their acute angles to n are equal; their tangential components have opposite
signs as geometric branches, yielding the opposite-side statement. For v=-n,
the outgoing direction is n, so the ray returns along the same line.

The angles a ray makes with the tangent plane are complementary to its normal
angles. Equality of those complementary angles can describe the same geometric
reflection, but calling them the standard incidence/reflection angles is the
wrong measurement convention. A candidate must distinguish the named-angle
definition from that mathematically equivalent reparameterization.
On a curved mirror the normal is local; it is not one fixed global direction.
No frequency, amplitude, material-dependent reflectivity or refraction law is
required or inferred by this geometric source task.

## Q2: Full Independent Principal-Focus Solution

The source asks for the principal focus of a spherical mirror, not only a
concave-mirror focus. State both mirror types with the approximation made clear:

- Consider rays close to and initially parallel to the principal axis, in the
  paraxial approximation. The principal focus is the axial point where their
  reflected paths, or the backward extensions of those paths, meet in that
  model. It is not the common point for arbitrary off-axis or wide beams.
- For a concave mirror those reflected rays converge in front of the mirror,
  on the incident-light side. Their actual intersection is a real principal
  focus.
- For a convex mirror those reflected rays diverge in front of the mirror.
  Their backward extensions meet behind the mirror, giving a virtual principal
  focus. Actual reflected rays do not travel through that behind-mirror point.

Generic independent small-angle check, not an unseen source diagram: for a
spherical radius R and a small height y, the local normal tilts by approximately
y/R. Reflection changes the parallel ray direction by approximately twice that
tilt. Its axial crossing therefore satisfies y/f approximately 2y/R, giving a
focus distance of magnitude R/2 in the first-order model. Concave curvature
directs the crossing toward the incident side; convex curvature requires
backward extension on the opposite side.
The curvature center is at distance R from the pole, not R/2, so it must not be
identified with the principal focus. No numerical radius or unseen point is
supplied to learners; this calculation is internal reasoning only.

With finite aperture, spherical aberration prevents arbitrary parallel rays
from sharing one exact point. Therefore every candidate formulation retains
the paraxial restriction. "In front" is defined as the incident-light side, so
real/virtual location does not depend on an unstated coordinate sign convention.
No convex/concave scope is silently omitted.
