# Electrical Energy Transmission

Electricity users are spread across different locations, while power stations may be built near fuel supplies or water resources.
The distance between stations and users creates a need for long-distance transmission; this does not mean that electrical energy can never be stored.
When current flows through a transmission conductor with resistance, some electrical energy is converted to heat.
For constant conductor resistance and a constant root-mean-square current, the Joule–Lenz law gives this energy as:
$Q=I^2Rt$.
Here Q is the electrical energy converted to heat, I is the root-mean-square current, and t is the elapsed time; for steady direct current, I is simply the constant current.

The quantity $R$ is the resistance of the transmission conductor.
For the same material and cross-sectional area, a longer conductor has greater resistance; the economic effect of its losses also depends on conditions such as the transmitted power.
Increasing the conductor's cross-sectional area reduces its resistance but requires more material and adds cost.
Reducing the transmission current is therefore an important way to reduce resistive heating losses.

In AC transmission, increasing the root-mean-square voltage reduces the root-mean-square current when the transmitted active power, power factor, and circuit configuration remain unchanged, thereby reducing resistive heating losses.
High voltage is often used for long-distance transmission, but its selection must also account for insulation, line construction, and cost.
As an example of voltage levels, consider a transmission voltage of $500$ kilovolts and a generator terminal voltage of $16$–$20$ kilovolts.
These values are not fixed standards for every system, and the latter range is not a universal upper limit on AC generator voltage.
Higher voltages place greater demands on the insulation of windings and other components.

A power station can use a step-up transformer to raise the generator's AC terminal voltage to a suitable transmission voltage.
For an ideal transformer with no losses, equal input and output power factors, and corresponding circuit connections, the output current is reduced relative to the input current by the same factor by which the output voltage is increased relative to the input voltage.
Real transformers have losses, including winding heating and core losses, so their output active power is less than their input active power; the ideal relationship is not an exact result that applies without conditions.

Lathe motors, lighting circuits, and other electrical equipment need voltages suited to their ratings, rather than the high voltage used for long-distance transmission.
At the receiving end of an AC supply, step-down transformers can reduce the voltage to an appropriate level.

Transmission voltage is often reduced in several stages, while the network branches to deliver electrical energy to users in different locations.
For the same transmitted power and power factor, a lower voltage corresponds to a higher current; each branch current also depends on its own load, so the current does not necessarily increase in every downstream conductor.

At high voltage, corona discharge may occur in the air near a conductor's surface, causing additional energy losses.
Corona depends on the electric field near the surface and is affected by factors such as conductor size and arrangement.
Selecting an AC line voltage requires attention to the peak voltage and suitable conductor and insulation design to control corona losses, rather than assuming that a high-voltage line has none.

High-voltage direct-current transmission is an option for transmitting electrical energy over long distances, but it is not superior to AC transmission under every set of conditions.
In a simplified comparison, if the permitted DC operating voltage can equal the peak AC voltage and the current limits and other constraints are comparable, a DC line may carry more power; using the same conductors alone does not guarantee this result.
Steady DC transmission avoids the inductive-reactance voltage drop and continuing capacitive charging current associated with AC lines, which matters for long-distance lines.
Inductance and capacitance still exist and affect changes in current or voltage; DC transmission also retains conductor, corona, and converter losses.

In one arrangement for connecting an AC power station to a DC transmission line, a transformer first adjusts the AC voltage, and a rectifier then converts AC to DC.
At the receiving end, an inverter converts DC back to AC, and a transformer adjusts the AC voltage to the required level.
Rectifiers and inverters are converter equipment; conversion requires suitable equipment, control, and insulation design and introduces losses, while an ordinary transformer cannot directly change a steady DC voltage.

Connecting power stations and users in different regions with high-voltage transmission lines can form an interconnected grid: a power system comprising generation, transmission, and electricity-use facilities.
By coordinating generation and transmission between regions, an interconnected grid can redistribute electrical supply when conditions permit and help manage demand peaks, such as those in the morning and evening.
It cannot eliminate every demand peak or guarantee delivery regardless of location and line conditions; supply remains limited by line capacity, losses, equipment operating conditions, and faults.
