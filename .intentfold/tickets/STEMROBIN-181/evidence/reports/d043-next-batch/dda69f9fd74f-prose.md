# Wave Phenomena

Dropping a stone into the still surface of a pond or lake produces rings of ripples that spread outward (Figure 64).
Waves breaking on a shore are another familiar example, and accounts of sea voyages often describe waves tossing even large ships.
The sound of splashing water reaches our ears as sound waves traveling through the air we breathe; the light that lets us see objects also has wave properties.

Wave phenomena are widespread in nature, although different waves can have very different causes.
As with oscillations, different types of waves can be described by the same or similar mathematical relationships under suitable conditions, without having the same physical mechanism or propagation speed.
Comparing water waves, sound waves, and light helps us understand both their similarities and their differences.

What is a wave?
How does a wave arise?

The particles in solids, liquids, and gases interact with one another.
When particles in one region begin to oscillate, these interactions can disturb neighboring regions in succession, allowing the oscillation pattern to propagate through the medium.
A particle's local motion is different from the outward propagation of the disturbance; the possible directions and speeds of propagation depend on the medium and the type of wave.

A wave is an oscillation or disturbance that propagates through space over time.

Within air, solids, and liquids, the restoring action involved in mechanical waves is related to the elasticity of the medium.
This elastic interaction links different parts of the material and allows a local disturbance to spread.
For waves on water, gravity and surface tension provide restoring actions; their relative importance depends on conditions such as the scale of the wave.

Waves on water make the main features of wave motion easy to see: ripples from a local disturbance can form rings that expand outward.
When the pattern is fairly regular and neighboring crests have similar wavelengths, the spacing between the rings is approximately equal; not all water waves have the same spacing.

A light floating object, such as a matchbox, can move locally near its original position with both vertical and horizontal components when there is no significant current or wind and the wave amplitude is small, rather than continually propagating outward like a crest.
The relative sizes of its vertical and horizontal motions depend on water depth, wavelength, and the object's response; wave-induced drift can also occur, so neither vertical dominance nor relatively small horizontal motion can be assumed in general.

As a wave propagates, the disturbance pattern spreads outward; the same group of particles does not travel all the way with that pattern.
For example, a stone disturbs one region of the water, neighboring regions are affected in succession, and the surface pattern spreads into the surrounding water.

In an approximate description that neglects currents and wave-induced drift, the surface shape travels while the water mainly moves near its local position.
Propagation of the pattern is therefore not the same as a bulk flow of water; this does not mean that real water waves can never accompany any transport of matter.

Wave speed is an important property of propagation; a disturbance and its energy are not transmitted instantaneously.
Imagine a seagull flying so that it stays above the same crest on the sea: its speed along the direction of propagation then equals the speed of that crest, not the velocity of the water particles.

In water-wave experiments suitable for direct observation, the pattern usually moves slowly enough for its motion to be followed.
Wave speed depends on the type of wave and the conditions in the medium; in a dispersive situation, crests of different wavelengths can travel at different speeds, and crest speed need not equal the speed of a wave packet or of energy transport.

Waves on a cord and a spring help illustrate transverse and longitudinal waves.
Fix one end of an elastic cord, hold it gently taut, and make the other end oscillate: a wave travels along the cord (Figure 65).
In a small-amplitude string model with approximately unchanged mass per unit length and other relevant conditions, greater tension gives a greater wave speed; stretching an elastic cord can also change its mass per unit length and other properties, so this is not a universal rule for every medium.

At the fixed end, the wave is reflected and the reflected disturbance travels back along the cord.
The changing shape propagates, while each small segment oscillates near its own equilibrium position.
For the sideways oscillations considered here, each segment moves perpendicular to the direction in which the wave propagates (Figure 66); a mechanical wave with this directional relationship is called a transverse wave.

Not every wave is transverse: particles can also oscillate back and forth along the direction of propagation (Figure 67), producing a longitudinal mechanical wave.
A long, flexible spring with a large coil diameter makes longitudinal waves easy to observe.
A tap from the palm at one end produces a compressed region that moves along the spring, forming an elastic pulse (Figure 68a).

Repeated taps can produce successive regions of compression and rarefaction that propagate along the spring (Figure 68b).
Each coil mainly oscillates along the spring near its own equilibrium position; a moving region of compression or rarefaction is not the same coil traveling the whole distance.

Sound is a particularly important mechanical wave and requires a material medium such as a gas, liquid, or solid; sound in air is mainly a longitudinal disturbance consisting of compressions and rarefactions.
The particle motion in sound waves is harder to observe and study directly than the visible deformations of a cord or a spring.

A wave receives energy from its source; for example, an external action that makes a cord or string oscillate supplies energy to the medium.
As the wave propagates, local motion and deformation affect other parts of the material in succession, allowing energy to pass from one region to another.
Energy transport by a wave does not require the particles of the medium to migrate with the wave as a whole; this distinguishes energy propagation from matter transport without denying that real waves can accompany drift or currents.

For an elastic cord, the mechanical energy includes the kinetic energy of its moving segments and the potential energy of elastic deformation.
On a real cord, dissipation can convert some mechanical energy into internal energy, attenuating the wave so that its amplitude usually decreases with propagation distance; this lossy case is not a rule that every wave must attenuate.

Mechanical waves require a material medium and can propagate through suitable gases, liquids, or solids; sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
Electromagnetic waves do not require a material medium, and both radio waves and light are electromagnetic waves.
An electromagnetic field can exist in a vacuum, a region containing no atoms or other material particles, so electromagnetic waves can propagate there.

Although their physical mechanisms differ fundamentally from those of mechanical waves, electromagnetic waves can also carry disturbances and energy; this transport is not a bulk movement of matter along with the wave.
Their disturbances and energy do not propagate instantaneously, but this does not imply that all waves have the same speed in different media or at different frequencies, or that crest speed in a dispersive medium always equals the speed of energy transport.
