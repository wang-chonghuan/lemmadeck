# 36. Soviet Electrification: Achievements and Prospects

The source textbook reports that the State Plan for the Electrification of Russia was adopted in 1920, and describes it as the first long-term national economic development plan in human history based on electrification.

It identifies V. I. Lenin as the initiator and advocate of this plan.
Lenin regarded electrification as decisive for transforming the national economy;

this is the historical position recorded by the textbook.

The source textbook quotes V. I. Lenin: "Only when the country has been electrified, and only when the technological basis of modern large-scale industry has been introduced into industry, agriculture and transport, can we achieve final victory."

This quotation expresses Lenin's political and economic judgment about electrification and economic transformation, rather than a law of physics.

The source textbook interprets the preceding half-century of development as evidence of the strength of Lenin's ideas;
this is its historical assessment.

It reports that, compared with 1913, the State Plan for the Electrification of Russia called for industrial production to double and electricity production to rise to 4 times the baseline.

According to the textbook, all targets had been exceeded after 10 years, ahead of the stipulated deadline.

It also reports that the pace of electrification increased year by year and that, by 1947, the Soviet Union ranked first in Europe and second in the world in electricity production;

these are historical rankings reported by the source, not current rankings.

The source textbook reports that, in the period it describes, the Soviet Union produced nearly five times as much electrical energy in a day as it had produced during the whole of 1921, which it identifies as the first year of the national electrification plan.

This comparison concerns total electrical energy, not generating power;
the source's word "now" refers to its historical period, not the present day.

---

The source textbook reports that hydropower accounted for about $20\%$ of the Soviet Union's electricity production at the time.

It describes the Krasnoyarsk hydroelectric station, named to commemorate 50 years since the founding of the Soviet Union, as the world's largest hydroelectric station at that time.

The textbook reports that the station had hydroelectric generating units rated at 500000 kilowatts, which it describes as the most powerful such units in the world at the time.

It gives the station's total power as six million kilowatts;

these capacities and superlatives belong to the source's historical report and are not statements of current capacity or ranking.

The source textbook reports that thermal power stations were also being developed toward greater power.

It states that many Soviet thermal power stations at the time exceeded one million kilowatts, and that many of these exceeded two million kilowatts.

It gives the power of the Krivorozhsk thermal power station as three million kilowatts and describes it as the world's largest thermal power station at that time.

These are the historical capacities and ranking reported by the textbook, not a comparison of present-day power stations.

The source textbook describes large nuclear power stations as playing an increasingly important role in electric power engineering at the time.

It reports that a resolution of the twenty-fifth congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union called for priority development of nuclear energy in the European part of the Soviet Union.

This passage reports the policy and development direction of that period, not present-day energy policy or a technical conclusion applicable to every region.

---

The source textbook reports that a unified all-Soviet power system was being formed by linking the power systems of Siberia and Central Asia with those in the European part of the Soviet Union.

It presents this interconnection as a way to improve the reliability and flexibility of supply across the country's extensive territory;

the actual effect depends on the grid and its operating conditions.
It also reports that this task required transmission trunk lines at 500000 volts, 750000 volts and 1150000 volts.

These voltages and construction directions are part of the historical planning described by the source, not a claim that all such lines had already been completed at the time.
