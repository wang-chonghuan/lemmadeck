19. Gas Properties Used in Engineering

The compressibility of gases, their adjustable pressure, and their response to temperature changes are useful in many engineering devices.

State equation (2-19) can explain many of the gas properties discussed in this lesson.
Here we use the ideal-gas approximation;
it does not describe real gases at every pressure, temperature, or phase.

**The elastic action of compressed gas**
Within the ideal-gas approximation, the pressure of a gas on the container walls is

$p=\frac{m}{\mu}\cdot\frac{RT}{V}$.
(2-23)

Consider the formal limits $m\to0$, $V\to\infty$, or $T\to0$ separately in this model, keeping the other relevant quantities appropriately finite;
the pressure approaches zero.

As a gas becomes more rarefied, the pressure on the walls can become very small, but pumping out gas does not mean removing every molecule.
The low-temperature limit also goes beyond the range of the classical ideal-gas model;
the last limit must not be interpreted as saying that the molecules of real matter necessarily become completely motionless at absolute zero.

The force of a gas on a wall is $F=pS$, where the area is that of the surface receiving the force, and the pressure on that surface is assumed uniform.
Compressing a confined gas generally raises its pressure, producing resistance to compression somewhat like a spring.

Unlike a solid spring, a gas does not retain a fixed shape;
it occupies the available space connected to it.
This analogy does not mean that a gas follows a linear spring law in every process.

**Adjusting gas pressure**
Changing a gas volume or temperature can change its pressure.

For the same gas species, if volume and temperature remain fixed, pressure is also proportional to the gas mass (see 2-23).
Adding gas to a container or releasing some of it can therefore adjust the pressure.

Inflating a car tire or a soccer ball is a familiar example, although inflation can also change the volume and temperature.
Equipment must operate within its material-strength limits and permitted conditions;
an ideal-gas formula does not imply that a container can withstand arbitrary pressure.

Compression pumps and compressors can compress gas and supply it to a container.
A piston compressor has basic structural similarities to a common piston water pump, but not all compressors have the same detailed construction.

**Gas compressibility**
At ordinary temperatures and pressures near atmospheric pressure, gases are easier to compress than liquids and solids.

The same pressure change will often produce a more noticeable volume change in a gas.
However, the pressure change also depends on the initial volume, the amount of gas, and the temperature conditions of the process;
a large volume change does not always produce only a small pressure change.

Figure $26$ shows an isotherm for a fixed amount of gas.
When the initial volume $V_1$ is larger, the same decrease in volume $\Delta V$ corresponds, within the range shown, to a smaller pressure increase $\Delta p_1$.

When the initial volume $V_2$ is smaller, the same decrease in volume $\Delta V$ corresponds to a larger pressure increase $\Delta p_2$.
If a confined gas is compared with a compressed spring, its resistance to compression is therefore not constant.
Along this isotherm, greater compression makes the pressure increase caused by the same further compression larger.

A gas can do work on its surroundings by pushing a movable boundary such as a piston;
when a gas is compressed, its surroundings can do work on it.

A gas can change its volume relatively easily, allowing it to transmit force over a longer stroke.
Only with suitable temperature conditions, a large gas reservoir, a continuous gas supply, or comparable conditions can the pressure change during operation be small.

**Temperature effects on pressure and volume**
For a fixed amount of gas, heating at fixed volume raises the pressure;
heating at fixed pressure increases the volume.

These correspond respectively to the laws called Charles's law and Gay-Lussac's law in the original textbook;
the fixed-volume and fixed-pressure conditions must not be combined.

Using the pressure or volume at Celsius zero as the reference, and retaining the textbook ideal-gas approximation, the pressure temperature coefficient $\gamma$ and volume expansion coefficient $\alpha$ are written as
$\gamma=\alpha=\frac{1}{273}$ degrees$^{-1}$.

The original textbook describes these coefficients as several hundred times larger than the corresponding coefficients of liquids and solids;
the actual ratio depends on the materials and temperature range being compared.

**A small amount of gas can produce appreciable pressure**
At a suitable volume and temperature, a small mass of gas can still produce appreciable pressure.

In an ordinarily rarefied gas, molecular attraction is insufficient to keep the molecules packed closely together, so they can move within the container.
Molecules collide with the container walls and exchange momentum, producing gas pressure.
