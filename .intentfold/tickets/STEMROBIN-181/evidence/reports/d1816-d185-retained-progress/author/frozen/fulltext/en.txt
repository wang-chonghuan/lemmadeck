Real circuits have resistance and other losses, so free electrical oscillations decay; if they decay rapidly, they are unsuitable as a means of supplying power continuously.
Forced electrical oscillations receive energy continually from an external source and can maintain their amplitude in a steady state, making them important in practice.

The original book uses alternating current in lighting circuits and factories as examples of forced electrical oscillations maintained by an external source.
This lesson uses a sinusoidal alternating-current model: the voltage varies harmonically, and the steady-state response of a linear circuit is a current that varies harmonically at the same angular frequency.
Not every alternating current has a sinusoidal waveform, and the current in a nonlinear load need not remain sinusoidal.

The original book uses an electronic oscilloscope to illustrate the waveform of a sinusoidal voltage (Figure $32$): in the cathode-ray-tube apparatus it describes, a signal corresponding to the lighting-circuit voltage acts on the vertical deflection plates to deflect the electron beam vertically, while a sawtooth sweep voltage moves the bright spot horizontally across the screen, producing a sinusoidal voltage-versus-time trace.
The sawtooth frequency and other settings selected by the experimenter determine the horizontal sweep speed; with a calibrated time scale, the time occupied by a complete cycle determines the oscillation frequency.
This is a description of a historical apparatus, not a mains-measurement procedure; students must not connect an oscilloscope or other wires directly to a live outlet.

The example in the original book has a frequency of 50 hertz, meaning that the corresponding number of complete cycles occurs each second.
For the sinusoidal current discussed here, each second contains 50 half-cycles of flow in one direction and 50 half-cycles in the opposite direction; frequency counts complete cycles, not each reversal of direction.
The original book also reports that many countries at the time used 50 hertz for industrial alternating current, while others, including the United States, used 60 hertz; this retains the source's historical account rather than verifying present-day national standards.

For an ohmic conductor whose material properties are approximately constant, in an applicable linear, quasistatic model, a sinusoidal voltage produces a harmonically varying electric field and an oscillating mean directed velocity of the charge carriers.
This directed motion is drift motion, not the electrons' entire microscopic velocity.

When the time needed for the carriers to respond is much shorter than the oscillation period, the instantaneous drift velocity is approximately proportional to the local electric field at that instant, so current and voltage in a purely resistive element are approximately in phase.
As with direct current, alternating current depends on the applied voltage, but it also depends on the circuit's resistance, inductance, and capacitance; the instantaneous proportionality for a pure resistance cannot be extended to every alternating-current circuit.

When the voltage across a circuit changes, changes in the electric field propagate along the circuit at a high but finite speed, rather than occurring everywhere at exactly the same instant; the original book had introduced this point in ninth grade.
Only when the propagation time along the circuit is much shorter than the voltage oscillation period can this delay be neglected in a quasistatic, lumped-circuit approximation.

Within that approximation, if current differences caused by charge accumulation along the conductors are also negligible, the current through each cross-section of the same unbranched series circuit is approximately equal at a given instant.
This conclusion does not apply to circuits of arbitrary length or frequency, and it does not mean that electrons cross the entire circuit instantaneously.

The original book attributes the alternating voltage across a lighting-circuit outlet to generators at power stations; here we discuss the generating principle, not operations on an outlet.
Section 17 of the original book introduced a simple alternating-current generator model that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy.

Consider a single-turn, planar wire loop in a uniform magnetic field, with area $S$ and magnetic flux $\Phi$ through it.
Choose a normal direction perpendicular to the loop's plane; $\alpha$ is the angle between this normal and the magnetic induction vector, not the angle between the plane and the field (Figure $33$).
With B denoting the magnitude of the magnetic induction, the signed magnetic flux through the loop is:
$\Phi=BS\cos\alpha$

Use the simple uniform-rotation model in which the loop normal sweeps a plane containing the magnetic-field direction, and choose the time origin at the position where the normal points along the field.
In the equation below, the angle $\alpha$ is the rotation angle accumulated continuously from that initial position, or rotational phase; it is not the principal geometric angle, which remains between zero and half a turn.
The cosine of this continuously accumulated rotation angle equals the cosine of the geometric angle between the loop normal and B, so this rotation-phase convention can be used in the preceding flux formula.
Under this convention, the rotation angle increases uniformly with time t, with the angular frequency denoted by $\omega$:
$\alpha=\omega t$
Here the magnitude of the rotational angular velocity is the angular frequency of the flux oscillation, measured in radians per second; ordinary frequency f counts cycles per second, and angular frequency is frequency multiplied by twice pi.

In this model, with magnetic-field magnitude, loop area, and rotational angular speed all constant, substituting the time dependence of the angle gives a harmonically varying, cosine-form magnetic flux:
$\Phi=BS\cos\omega t$

Figure 33

By the law of electromagnetic induction, the induced emf around a single-turn loop is the negative time derivative of the signed magnetic flux; the positive direction for emf corresponds to the chosen normal by the right-hand rule.
A prime denotes differentiation with respect to time, and the minus sign expresses the direction in which induction opposes the change in flux; it must not be dropped.
Differentiating the preceding cosine function gives:
$e=-\Phi'=-BS(\cos\omega t)'=BS\omega\sin\omega t=\mathcal{E}_м\sin\omega t$
With this time origin and direction convention, the flux has cosine form and the induced emf has sine form; their phase relationship follows from the negative derivative.

Here $\mathcal{E}_м=BS\omega$ is the amplitude of the induced emf, its maximum absolute value during a cycle.
B, S, and the magnitude of the angular frequency are taken as nonnegative; the amplitude is distinct from the instantaneous emf that changes sign with time.

Next we study forced electromagnetic oscillations maintained by an externally applied sinusoidal voltage, using the steady-state model of a linear circuit.
The voltage varies harmonically at angular frequency $\omega$; ordinary frequency f is measured in hertz, whereas angular frequency is measured in radians per second, so the terms must not be confused.
Choose a cosine representation for the voltage and set its initial phase to zero:

$u=U_м\cos\omega t$ (2.12)

Here $U_м$ is the voltage amplitude, the maximum absolute voltage①.
The symbol u denotes instantaneous voltage at a particular time; the amplitude is not the rms value.

① For the same harmonic voltage, using a cosine rather than a sine representation corresponds to changing the initial phase used by $\frac{\pi}{2}$.
The original book's statement that this is immaterial means that the time origin and a consistent phase representation can be chosen without changing the physical forced oscillation.
It does not mean that the relative phase of current and voltage can be ignored: when the time origin changes, both phases must change together, leaving their phase difference unchanged.

In a linear circuit whose parameters do not vary with time, a sinusoidal voltage drives the circuit at angular frequency $\omega$; once free oscillations and other transients have decayed, the steady-state current varies harmonically at that same angular frequency.
This is not a conclusion that applies to every alternating waveform or nonlinear circuit.

The current need not have the same phase as the voltage, just as the velocity in forced mechanical oscillations generally has a different phase from the driving force.
Thus, within this steady-state model, the current $i$ at any instant, that is, the instantaneous current, can be written as:

$i=I_м\cos(\omega t+\varphi)$ (2.13)

Here $I_м$ is the current amplitude, the maximum absolute current; $\varphi$ is the phase of the current minus the phase of the voltage.
Because the voltage's initial phase is zero, the plus sign in the current formula fixes the sign convention: a positive phase difference means current leads voltage, a negative difference means it lags, and zero difference means they are in phase, with phases understood modulo a complete cycle.
The current amplitude is not the rms value, and changing both sine and cosine representations together cannot eliminate the current-voltage phase difference.
