# Dispersion of Light

First keep the material and its condition fixed, and consider an ordinary transparent material with the same properties throughout and in every direction.
In this simple model, changing only the angle at which a beam enters does not change the material's **refractive index** for that colour.
A different colour can have a different refractive index, and Newton (牛顿) discovered and studied this difference.

While improving a telescope, Newton (牛顿) noticed coloured edges around the image formed by its objective lens, the lens at the front that forms the image.
These coloured edges interested him, and he began studying light of different colours carefully.

Newton's epitaph praised his pioneering study of the variety of light rays and the different colours associated with them.
It also described these discoveries as something people at the time had not expected.

Before Newton, however, people had already seen rainbow colours around images formed by lenses and around objects viewed through a prism.
Beams passing through a prism also showed coloured edges, so these older observations raised a question that experiments could investigate.

Newton (牛顿) used a simple experiment in which sunlight passed through a small hole in a window shutter, making a thin beam in a dark room.
After entering a glass prism, the beam was refracted and made an elongated patch of rainbow colours on the opposite wall, as shown in Figure 196.

For many centuries, people had traditionally divided the rainbow into seven basic colours, and Newton also used seven colour names.
The source lists them in this order: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange-yellow, and red.

Newton called this seven-colour band a **spectrum**.
The names help us describe the band, but neighbouring colours in the actual sunlight spectrum change gradually rather than forming only a few separate colours.

When Newton (牛顿) covered the hole with red glass, he saw only a red patch on the wall.
When he used blue glass, he saw a blue patch, and other coloured pieces of glass could be compared in the same way.

These observations showed that the old idea that a prism dyes light was mistaken.
In this ordinary-glass experiment, the prism separates colours already mixed in the light instead of making new colours, and the source retains the reference 彩图 I (Color Plate I) for this step.

White light is not just one colour, and many different colours can be separated from it.
When these colours enter our eyes together, their combined effect makes us see white light.

The source then uses another prism turned through $180^\circ$ relative to the first and, with a suitable arrangement, brings all the beams in the spectrum back together.
The result is white light again, linked in the source to 彩图 II (Color Plate II), because all the colours have been recombined, not because turning a prism alone always works.

If sufficiently single-coloured green light is selected and passed through another ordinary glass prism, it remains green rather than changing into another colour through refraction.
The condition matters, because a selected green band that still contains nearby colours can have those finer differences separated further.

In Opticks (《光学》), Newton (牛顿) explained that different colours are refracted by different amounts, meaning that glass has different refractive indices for them.
For the ordinary glass and visible light discussed here, violet has the greatest refractive index and red the smallest, which describes the usual behaviour of that glass.
**The dependence of refractive index on the colour of light is called dispersion**, but the red-to-violet ordering just described is not a rule for every material over every colour range.

To distinguish the colours more clearly, Newton (牛顿) improved the experiment for observing a spectrum.
The circular patches of different colours made with the round hole partly overlapped, and the source refers again to Figure 196.

He replaced the hole with a narrow slit lit by a bright source, then placed a lens behind the slit to form its image as a narrow white strip on a screen.
When a prism was placed in the light path, the image of the slit spread into a spectrum, as shown in Figure 197.

In the glass just discussed, red light has a smaller refractive index, is refracted less, and travels fastest in the glass, while violet light travels slowest.
Here the speed means how fast the same stage of the light wave's vibration advances, called its phase speed, which can differ between colours.

Refractive index is related to this speed, so ordinary glass refracts different colours by different amounts and separates white light.
In a vacuum, different colours of light travel at the same speed, so the ordering in glass must not be carried over to a vacuum.

The source illustrates this with Roemer (罗默) observing Jupiter's (木星) satellite $1$, imagining that unequal vacuum speeds would make it look reddish just as it left the shadow and violet just before it entered, although no such effect was seen.
This retains the source's historical explanation, but the absence of such a colour change is a related observation, not by itself a precise measurement of vacuum light speed for all colours.

Knowing that white light contains many colours helps explain the colours of paper, grass, and leaves, and here we consider ordinary objects that neither emit light nor change incoming light into another colour.
When white light falls on white paper, the paper reflects the different colours in roughly their incoming proportions, so it looks white.

Adding a red coating does not create new red light, but changes which incoming colours can return to our eyes.
The source describes this as reflecting only red and absorbing the other colours, while an ordinary real red coating mainly reflects red and absorbs more of the other colours.

In sunlight, grass and leaves mainly reflect green light and absorb more of the other colours, which makes them look green.
The source's phrase about reflecting only green simplifies this process rather than claiming that real leaves reflect no other colour at all.

It then considers ideal red glass that transmits only red light and uses it to look at grass.
The green light reflected by the grass can hardly pass through that glass, so little light reaches the eyes and the grass looks almost black, with 'almost' allowing for colours that are not perfectly pure.
