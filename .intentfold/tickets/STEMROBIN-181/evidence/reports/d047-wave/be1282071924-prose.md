# 39. Wavelength and Wave Speed

Once the transverse wave reaches the thirteenth ball, it and the first ball vibrate identically.
Both pass equilibrium moving left together (Figure 70e).

After another $\frac{1}{4}$ of a period, both reach their maximum leftward displacement (Figure 70f).
Their phase is always the same.

**Wavelength is the distance between nearest in-phase points**.
The first and thirteenth, second and fourteenth, and third and fifteenth balls share this spacing (Figure 70e, f).

For the longitudinal wave in Figure 71b, a wavelength separates the second and fourteenth balls or the fourth and sixteenth.
Wavelength is denoted by the Greek letter $\lambda$.

Phase differs except across $n\lambda$ (integer $n$).
Balls one and seven, $\lambda/2$ apart, are in antiphase: one moves left as the other moves right, and vice versa (Figure 70).

In one period, a wave travels a distance $\lambda$ (see Figure 70e).
Its speed is therefore given by
$v=\frac{\lambda}{T}$ (4.1)

Since the period $T$ and frequency $\nu$ are related by
$T=\frac{1}{\nu}$,
it follows that:
$v=\lambda\nu$ (4.2)

**Wave speed equals wavelength multiplied by the vibration frequency**.

When a wave travels along a string, we can observe two kinds of periodicity.

Each point on the string repeats its motion periodically.
For harmonic sine or cosine motion, all points have the same frequency and amplitude, but differ in phase.

At any instant, the profile repeats at intervals of $\lambda$.
Figure 72 shows this profile as the curve through $O$.
The whole shape moves rightward at speed $v$ as time passes.

Equation (4.2), which relates wave speed, wavelength, and vibration frequency, also applies to longitudinal waves.
