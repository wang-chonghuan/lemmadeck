# Propagation of Mechanical Waves

To understand wave propagation, we need to follow individual particles of the medium.
We begin with a transverse wave traveling along an elastic cord.

Every short segment of the cord has both mass and elasticity.
When the cord bends, elastic forces act across its cross sections.

The net restoring force tends to pull each segment toward equilibrium.
Inertia carries a moving segment past its equilibrium position.

Beyond equilibrium, the restoring force slows its outward motion.
At maximum displacement it stops briefly, then starts back.

Figure 69 models the cord with a chain of identical metal balls.
The balls hang from strings and are joined by springs.
The springs have much less mass than the balls.

Most of the model's mass is concentrated in the balls.
The light springs provide elastic coupling.
This captures the basic wave mechanism, not every detail of a real cord.

Move the leftmost ball sideways in the horizontal plane.
Here, sideways means perpendicular to the chain.
The connecting spring deforms and pulls the second ball sideways.

That ball has inertia, so its response takes time.
Its motion follows the first ball's motion with a delay.
We use a small-amplitude model, before appreciable loss or reflection.

Drive the first ball periodically with period $T$, by hand or a mechanism.
The second ball is forced to oscillate, with a phase delay.

Through the next spring, the third ball responds with a further delay.
The disturbance continues from ball to ball along the chain.

After the initial transient, the driven balls share the driving frequency.
Before reflected waves return, this is an outgoing transverse wave.
