1. Uniform, isotropic, unconstrained thermal expansion multiplies every length
   by the same factor. The two sector boundaries retain their directions and
   the missing sector angle phi is unchanged. A nonuniform temperature,
   anisotropy, or constraint would require separate analysis.
2. The liquid level rises when the liquid's volume expansion exceeds the
   container's internal capacity expansion. To first order the apparent excess
   volume is V0 * (gamma_liquid - gamma_container) * DeltaT. Vessel expansion
   alone cannot explain a rising level; a sufficiently greater vessel
   coefficient could instead reverse it. This is a historical mercury
   thermometer model, not advice to handle mercury or heat sealed glass.
3. At equal capillary cross-sectional area and equal material coefficients,
   dh/dT is proportional to the liquid reservoir volume (with vessel expansion
   included in the effective coefficient). The larger reservoir is more
   sensitive. Equal capillary diameters alone are insufficient if different
   materials or other operating conditions are allowed.
4. Initial hoop diameter D0 = 1.000 - 0.005 = 0.995 m. For the stated constant
   linear coefficient beta = 12e-6 K^-1, DeltaT = (1.000 - 0.995) /
   (0.995 * beta) = about 418.76 K, equivalently about 419 degrees Celsius of
   temperature rise. The common small-strain approximation using 1.000 m
   instead gives 416.67 K, about 417 K; the reference length must be declared.
   This is a mathematical fit calculation, not a heating/assembly procedure.
5. Equal volumes at 0 and 60 degrees Celsius give rho0/rho60 = 88/83.
   For one fixed mass, V60/V0 = 88/83 = 1 + gamma * 60.
   Therefore gamma = (88/83 - 1)/60 = 5/4980 = about 1.004016e-3 K^-1.
   Dividing by 88 instead would improperly change the reference volume to V60.
   Gravity cancels. Gasoline heating is not an operational activity.
6. Under fully fixed length, constant E and beta, and linear elastic response,
   the compressive stress magnitude is E * beta * DeltaT. Its value at 25
   degrees Celsius is (25/10) * 3e7 = 7.5e7 Pa. This extrapolation requires no
   yielding, creep, buckling, or wall motion; the stress is compressive.
7. Source fig-102 actually shows a strip fixed at its right end, an upper
   contact touching its left/free end, two horizontal layers, a heater in
   series below, and an AC source at left. To open the upper contact as the
   strip warms, the free end bends downward. The lower layer is the shorter
   inner side of that bend and has the smaller expansion coefficient; the
   upper layer has the larger coefficient. The opened series path interrupts
   the heater; cooling restores contact, giving switching about a temperature
   band rather than mathematically exact constant temperature. To make warming
   close a signal contact, place an initially separated sensing contact below
   the moving end, so its downward motion closes that contact. This is a
   conceptual change in a schematic, not instructions to modify mains wiring.
